Does Reading Before Bed Help You Sleep?

Reading before bed helps most people fall asleep more easily, because it gives a busy mind something to do that is absorbing but not arousing. The details matter: what you read, on what, and where.

By the Quiet Mind Sleep editorial team · Published 25 September 2026 · 5 min read

In this guide

  2. Paper or screen?
  3. What to read
  4. In bed or in a chair?
  5. Fitting it into the evening
  6. Where to go next

For most people, yes. Reading before bed is one of the most widely recommended wind-down activities, and for good reason: it occupies the mind fully enough to crowd out the day's worries, without the stimulation of a screen, a conversation or a task. It is also a strong cue. Do it every night and the act of opening a book starts to tell your body that sleep is next.

The evidence is thin but points the right way, the exceptions are predictable, and the three practical questions people ask are the right ones: paper or screen, what to read, and in bed or out of it.

Why reading helps

The problem with lying in the dark is that there is nothing to do except think, and thinking at night tends to run towards worry. Reading solves this by giving your attention somewhere to go. A good book takes you out of your own head, which is precisely what a mind that has been turning over the day needs.

It also lowers the effort. Trying to fall asleep is a reliable way to stay awake; reading until your eyes get heavy and then turning off the light lets sleep arrive as a by-product rather than a goal. That is the same logic that runs through all of our advice on a mind that won't switch off at night.

Direct evidence on reading specifically is limited. A large online trial run from Ireland in 2021, published in the journal Trials and known as The People's Trial, randomly assigned nearly a thousand volunteers to read a book in bed or not for a week; those who read reported slightly better sleep quality. It relied on self-report over a short period, so it is a nudge rather than proof, but it matches what most people find in practice.

Paper or screen?

Paper, if you can. A backlit phone or tablet held close to the face at full brightness is a light source, and a small but often-cited 2015 study by Chang and colleagues, published in the journal PNAS, found that people reading on a bright e-reader before bed produced less melatonin, took longer to fall asleep and felt less alert the next morning than when reading a printed book.

Two caveats keep this in proportion. That study used very bright settings for four hours, and the delay in falling asleep was around ten minutes. A screen turned right down, in night mode, is a much weaker stimulus. And the bigger problem with a phone in bed is not the light but what else is on it: a notification, a headline, a work email. Our guide to screens before bed weighs this properly.

If you prefer digital reading, an e-ink reader with the front light turned low is close to paper in its effect. If you use a tablet, dim it, warm it, and leave the other apps alone. If you use a phone, put it in flight mode first.

What to read

Absorbing but not gripping. The ideal bedtime book is one you enjoy enough to lose yourself in and can put down at the end of a chapter without needing to know what happens next. A thriller you cannot stop reading is a stimulant in paperback form. So is anything about work, money, the news or a subject you are already worrying about.

Fiction generally works better than non-fiction for this reason. Something you have read before is ideal: familiar enough to be soothing, involving enough to hold attention. Short stories, essays and poetry suit people who find that a novel keeps them up. Audiobooks do the same job for people who find reading in low light a strain, and let you keep the lights off; a sleep timer means you do not have to find your place again.

Reading the same thing every night, even a few pages, is worth more than a different, better book each week, because the repetition is what builds the cue.

In bed or in a chair?

This is the question that divides sleep advice, and the honest answer is that it depends on how well you sleep.

If you read in bed, drift off within twenty minutes of putting the book down and rarely lie awake, read in bed. It is comfortable, it is a lovely habit, and there is no reason to change it.

If you often lie awake, the standard advice from sleep clinics is to keep the bed for sleep and to do everything else, including reading, somewhere else. The logic is called stimulus control: the more time you spend awake in bed, the more the bed becomes a place where you are awake. Reading in a chair or on the sofa, in dim light, until you are sleepy, and then going to bed, keeps the association clean. It is the same principle behind getting up if you can't sleep.

A middle way that suits many people: read in bed, but if you notice you have been awake for a while after turning the light off, get up and read somewhere else until sleepy.

In short: Read something you enjoy but can put down, on paper or a dim e-ink screen, in a lamp-lit room. If you lie awake in bed, move the reading to a chair.

Fitting it into the evening

Reading works best as the final stage of a slightly longer wind-down: lights dimmed, the day closed off, the phone put away. Twenty minutes is plenty; some people manage five pages before their eyes give up, and that is the point. Our guide to a bedtime routine for adults shows where reading fits alongside the rest. A warm, low bedside lamp, of the kind covered in our guide to bedroom lighting, makes reading easy on the eyes without lighting up the room.

Where to go next

Tonight: pick a book you have read before, dim the lamp, and read until your eyes go rather than until the chapter ends. For more short answers to common questions, see the rest of our sleep questions.
