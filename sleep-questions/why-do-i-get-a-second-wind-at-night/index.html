Why Do I Get a Second Wind at Night?

The second wind is real: your body clock sends its strongest wake signal in the two or three hours before your natural sleep time. If you are trying to sleep early, or your clock runs late, that surge lands at bedtime.

By the Quiet Mind Sleep editorial team · Published 25 September 2026 · 5 min read

The second wind is not a quirk of your personality. It is a built-in feature of the body clock. In the last two or three hours before your natural sleep time, your clock sends out its strongest alerting signal of the day, strong enough to override the sleep pressure that has been building since morning. Sleep researchers call this stretch the wake maintenance zone, and some call it the forbidden zone for sleep, because falling asleep inside it is genuinely hard.

For someone whose natural sleep time is 11pm, the zone runs from about 8 or 9pm. That is a normal, useful thing: it keeps you going through the evening. The trouble starts when the zone lands later than your bedtime, either because you are trying to sleep early, or because your body clock runs late.

Why the body clock does this

Two systems decide when you sleep. Sleep pressure builds through the day the longer you are awake. The body clock, separately, sends an alerting signal that rises from morning, dips a little in the early afternoon, and then rises again to its peak in the evening before dropping off sharply as your natural sleep time approaches.

The evening peak exists precisely to counteract sleep pressure. Without it, you would get steadily sleepier from lunchtime onwards and be asleep by seven. Instead the two forces balance through the evening and then, when the alerting signal falls away, sleep pressure wins and you feel sleepy quite suddenly. That drop is what a good bedtime feels like. The second wind is what the hour before it feels like.

In short: The second wind is your body clock's evening alertness peak. It falls in the two or three hours before your natural bedtime, wherever that happens to be.

Reason 1: you are going to bed too early

If you feel sleepy at 8pm, then go to bed at 9:30pm and find yourself wide awake, you may have walked straight into the zone. The early sleepiness was the afternoon dip running late, or simple tiredness from the day; the alertness at 9:30pm is the clock doing its job. For most adults, trying to sleep more than about an hour before their usual time means fighting the strongest wake signal of the day.

This is one reason a fixed bedtime that is too early can backfire: you lie awake, get frustrated, and start to associate bed with being awake. It is one of the mechanisms behind feeling tired but unable to sleep.

Reason 2: your clock runs late

Some people are night owls by nature. Their body clocks are set later, so their evening alertness peak lands at 10pm or midnight and their natural sleep time is 1am or later. If you are one of them and you are trying to sleep at 11pm for work, you are trying to fall asleep at the peak of your own alerting signal, every night.

The same happens to anyone whose clock has drifted late through a run of late nights, weekend lie-ins or too much evening light. The peak moves with the clock, and the clock moves later easily and earlier only with effort.

Reason 3: you have pushed past your window

Overtiredness gives a different kind of second wind. If you ignore the moment of real sleepiness, say to finish something, the body responds with a small dose of stress hormones to keep you upright. The sleepiness passes, you feel oddly alert and even a little energised, and you may stay that way for an hour or more.

The tell is the sequence: a clear wave of sleepiness, ignored, followed by alertness. The clock version tends to feel more like ordinary evening wakefulness that simply does not fade.

Reason 4: your evening is doing it

Bright overhead lights, an intense series, a work email at 9:30pm, a heated conversation, a late gym session or a late coffee all push the alerting signal up or delay the clock. None of them creates the second wind on its own, but each one lengthens it.

What to do about it

Find your real bedtime. For a week, note when you feel genuinely sleepy in the evening: heavy eyes, yawning, losing your place in a book. That is your window. Go to bed then, not before. If it is later than you would like, that is the next step's job.

Move the clock earlier, gently. The alertness peak follows your body clock, and the strongest lever on the clock is daylight soon after waking, at the same time every day, including weekends. Morning light pulls the whole cycle earlier over a couple of weeks, which pulls the second wind earlier with it. Our guide to how your morning routine shapes tonight's sleep explains why the morning matters more than the evening. For a larger shift, fixing your sleep schedule walks through it week by week.

Dim the evening. Lamps rather than the big light from about two hours before bed, and screens turned down. Bright light in the evening tells the clock it is still afternoon. See our guide to bedroom lighting for sleep for the practical version.

Catch the wave. When sleepiness arrives, go. One more episode or one more scroll will carry you into the zone.

Use the zone rather than fighting it. If you know you are alert between 9 and 10:30pm, that is a good time for the quiet, useful things: the dishes, tomorrow's bag, a walk with the dog. It is not a good time to be lying in the dark, which is why it helps to have an idea of how long falling asleep should normally take, and to get up if it is taking much longer.

Keep naps short and early. A long or late nap takes sleep pressure away from the evening, leaving the alertness peak with nothing to push against.

When a second wind is something else

An occasional second wind is normal. A pattern of feeling alert late every night and unable to wake in the morning, which is affecting work or study, may be a body clock that is set persistently late, and it is worth raising with a GP, particularly for teenagers and young adults. A burst of energy at night that comes with a reduced need for sleep over several days, racing thoughts or unusually elevated mood is a different matter and should be discussed with a GP promptly.
