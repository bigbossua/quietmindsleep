Waking Up Too Early and Can't Get Back to Sleep

Early waking usually means your body clock is running ahead of your alarm, and the summer dawn, evening habits, age and mood all push it that way. Most of those can be nudged back.

Waking up too early, an hour or two before you need to, and then lying there unable to drift back, is one of the most frustrating sleep patterns there is. You are not short of sleep enough to feel dreadful, but you are never quite right either, and the last hour of the night becomes a slow wait for the alarm.

The most common reason is simple: your body clock has decided that morning starts at 5am, and it is behaving accordingly. The clock is set by light, by habit and by age, and each of those can be adjusted. Sometimes early waking has a different cause, low mood in particular, and this guide is clear about when that is worth a conversation with a GP.

What "too early" actually means

There is a difference between waking at 3am, which is usually a mid-sleep waking from light sleep, and waking at 5am or 5.30am and feeling done. The first is covered in the guide to waking up at 3am. This guide is about the second: sleep that ends early and does not restart.

Sleep specialists call this early morning awakening, and they consider it a problem when it happens most nights, leaves you well short of the sleep you need, and affects your days. Waking a little before the alarm now and then, or naturally rising at 6am after seven and a half hours, is not a problem. It is a body clock doing its job.

Why your body clock runs early

Light, and the UK summer

Your body clock resets itself every day using light, and morning light is the strongest signal it gets. Light in the early morning pulls the clock earlier; light in the evening pushes it later. From late April to early August in most of the UK, dawn arrives between about 4.30am and 5.30am, and a thin curtain lets enough of it through to tell your brain that the day has started. Many people who sleep fine in winter start waking early in May and cannot understand why.

The fix is to keep morning light out until you want to be awake. For most bedrooms that means a blackout blind or lined curtains that actually meet in the middle, or a sleep mask if you would rather not change the room. There is a room-by-room guide to making your bedroom darker if the light is getting in around the edges.

Too little light in the evening

The other half of the equation is evening. If your evenings are spent in a dim living room and you are in bed by 10pm, your clock gets no signal to hold back. Brighter (though still warm) lighting in the early evening, a walk after tea in summer, and simply staying up a little later can all nudge the clock towards a later morning. The guide to bedroom lighting for sleep covers the balance between enough light in the evening and too much at bedtime.

Going to bed too early

This is more common than people think. If you are in bed at 9.30pm because you are tired, and you need seven hours, you will be done by 4.30am. Early waking is then not a problem at all; it is arithmetic. Try moving bedtime later in 15-minute steps and see whether the morning follows.

Age

The body clock tends to drift earlier as we get older, deep sleep declines, and the second half of the night becomes lighter. Many people in their sixties and seventies find they wake naturally at 5am or 5.30am when they used to sleep until 7am. If you still feel reasonably refreshed, this is normal ageing rather than insomnia. The changes above still help, and a later bedtime often does more than anything.

An inconsistent schedule

Lie-ins at the weekend and early starts in the week keep the clock guessing, and a guessing clock tends to fire early. A consistent sleep schedule, with the same wake time seven days a week, is the single most effective thing for most early wakers, even though it sounds like the opposite of what you want.

Alcohol, and a full bladder

Both are more likely to wake you in the lighter, later part of the night. A drink in the evening fragments sleep as it wears off; a large mug of anything at 10pm has to come out at some point. Neither causes chronic early waking on its own, but both can tip a light sleeper over the edge.

In short: Early waking usually means your clock is set for an earlier morning than you want. Light, bedtime and consistency are what set it, so they are what move it.

Early waking and mood

Waking early, particularly an hour or two before you need to and then being unable to get back off, is a recognised feature of depression for some people. It is not the only sign, and plenty of people wake early for the ordinary reasons above and feel perfectly fine. But if early waking has arrived alongside any of the following, it is worth taking seriously:

  • feeling low, flat or empty most of the day, most days, for two weeks or more
  • losing interest or pleasure in things you would normally enjoy
  • feeling tired and slowed down, or restless and agitated
  • changes in appetite, difficulty concentrating, or feeling worthless or hopeless

If that sounds familiar, please talk to a GP. Depression is common and treatable, and improving the depression usually improves the sleep. You can also refer yourself directly to NHS talking therapies in England without going through a GP. There is a fuller guide to when to see a GP about sleep, and Mind has clear, kind information on both sleep and low mood.

If you ever feel that you cannot keep yourself safe, call 999, go to A&E, or call the Samaritans on 116 123 at any hour.

What to do when you wake early

If it is within an hour of your normal wake time and you feel reasonably alert, get up. Lying in bed trying to squeeze out another 45 minutes almost never works and teaches your brain that bed is a place for lying awake. Getting up, opening the curtains and starting the day gently is usually the better choice, and it strengthens the wake time you want.

If it is much earlier, stay dark and rest. Do not check the clock. Keep your eyes closed, breathe slowly, and give yourself permission to rest rather than sleep. If you become frustrated, take a short, dim break out of bed and return when you feel sleepy. The steps are laid out in the Waking at Night guides.

Do not nap to make up for it, and do not go to bed earlier the following night. Both undo the work of shifting the clock.

Shifting the clock later

If you want to move your natural wake time from 5am to 6.30am, work on these together for two to three weeks:

  1. Keep the bedroom properly dark until your chosen wake time. In summer this is the big one. Lined blackout curtains, a blind that seals at the edges, or a mask.
  2. Set a fixed wake time and hold it, even on the mornings you wake earlier and even at the weekend.
  3. Get more light in the evening, ideally daylight after work, and keep the living room reasonably bright until an hour before bed.
  4. Move bedtime later in small steps if you have been going to bed early.
  5. Keep morning light off your face until you are up. No phone in bed; open the curtains only when you get up.

If summer dawn is the clear trigger, a blackout solution is the highest-return change. The short version

Early waking is a body clock that thinks morning has come. Darken the room until your real wake time, get up at that time every day, get more light in the evening, and push bedtime later if it has crept early. Give it three weeks. If it is not moving, or if low mood has come with it, see your GP.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I wake up at 5am every day even without an alarm?

Most often because your body clock has settled on 5am as morning. That happens when you have been getting up early for a while, when you get a lot of morning light and little in the evening, and as you get older, since the clock tends to run earlier with age. A consistent, slightly later wake time and more evening light can shift it.

Is waking up too early a sign of depression?

It can be one feature of depression, particularly waking an hour or two before you need to and being unable to get back off, night after night. It is not a sign on its own. If early waking comes with persistent low mood, loss of interest or energy, or feeling hopeless, it is worth talking to a GP.

Should I just get up when I wake early?

If it is within about an hour of your normal wake time and you feel reasonably alert, getting up is often the better choice: lying in bed trying to force another hour rarely works. If it is much earlier than that, stay dark and rest, and get up for a short, dim break if you become frustrated.
