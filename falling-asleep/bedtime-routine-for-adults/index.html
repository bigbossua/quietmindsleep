A Bedtime Routine for Adults That You'll Actually Keep

A good adult bedtime routine is not a checklist of twelve wellness habits. It is about an hour, in three loose phases, repeated often enough that your body learns what it means.

A bedtime routine for adults works for the same reason it works for children: repetition turns a set of ordinary actions into a signal. Do roughly the same things, in roughly the same order, at roughly the same time, and after a couple of weeks your body starts to get sleepy on cue rather than by accident.

The routine most people fail to keep is the one built from a wellness article: journal, stretch, meditate, herbal tea, no screens, gratitude list, skincare, read. It is too long and it feels like homework. The one you will keep has three loose phases, takes about an hour, and bends around real life.

This guide builds that routine, then adapts it for shift work, parents and people who finish the day late. It sits within our Falling Asleep guides, which cover what to do when the routine is in place and sleep still will not come.

Why an hour, and why three phases

The hour before bed is when your body is meant to be shifting gear: core temperature starting to fall, the stress hormone cortisol at its low point, the sleep-promoting signal from your body clock rising. Bright light, engaging tasks and unresolved worries all hold that shift back. A routine is simply a way of not doing those things, reliably.

Three phases because different things need to wind down at different points:

  1. Close the day (first 20 minutes): admin, decisions and worries.
  2. Slow the body (middle 20 minutes): light, temperature, physical pace.
  3. Settle the mind (last 20 minutes): one quiet activity, then bed.

The times are a guide; what matters is the order and the direction of travel.

Phase 1: close the day (about 20 minutes)

This phase is about ending the day's business so that it does not follow you to bed.

Do the small jobs that would otherwise nag. Bag by the door, lunch in the fridge, tomorrow's first task written down. Five minutes here saves twenty minutes of mental rehearsal later.

Write a short list. Anything on your mind, and the next step for each item. This is not a diary and it does not need to be neat. Research on "constructive worry" suggests that doing this earlier in the evening reduces the amount of worrying people do once in bed. Our guide to writing a worry list before bed explains the method.

Set the last screen boundary. This is the moment to send the last message and put the phone somewhere that is not the bedside table. The evidence on screens and sleep is more modest than the headlines suggest, but the combination of bright light and a feed that never ends is a reliable way to postpone sleepiness. If you want the honest picture, see how much phones really affect sleep.

Have a warm drink if you like one. Caffeine-free tea or warm milk work because they are a ritual, not because of what is in them.

Phase 2: slow the body (about 20 minutes)

Dim the lights. Turn off the overhead light and use lamps. Warm-toned bulbs are better than cool white. This one change tells your body clock more than almost anything else you can do in the evening.

Warm up, then cool down. A warm bath or shower an hour or two before bed sounds like it should keep you warm, but the opposite happens: warm water draws blood to the skin and your core temperature drops afterwards, which is the state your body wants for sleep. A 2019 review in Sleep Medicine Reviews found this shortened the time to fall asleep by about ten minutes on average. If a bath is not practical, warm feet (socks, a hot water bottle) in a cool room does some of the same work.

Move gently or not at all. Some slow stretches or a short walk around the flat are fine. A hard workout is not; it raises heart rate and body temperature exactly when you need them to fall.

Set the room. Window on the latch or the heating off, curtains drawn, the bed made. A room around 16–19°C suits most people. If you are doing this at the same time each night, it becomes part of the signal too.

Phase 3: settle the mind (about 20 minutes)

One quiet activity, not three. Reading a physical book, listening to something slow, a simple craft, a relaxation practice. Choose whatever holds your attention gently without getting you invested. A gripping thriller keeps people up; a book you have read before does not.

A relaxation technique if you want one. Progressive muscle relaxation (tensing and releasing muscle groups from the feet up) has the longest track record in insomnia research and is easy to learn from a step-by-step script. Slow breathing with a longer out-breath is simpler still. Neither needs to be done perfectly.

Go to bed when you feel sleepy. Not when the routine ends. If the heaviness has not arrived, keep reading in low light until it does. Getting into bed wide awake because it is 10.30 teaches your body that bed is a place to lie awake.

In short: Close the day on paper, dim the lights and cool the room, then give your mind one gentle thing to do until sleepiness arrives.

Sample routines for real lives

If you work shifts

Your routine has to travel with you, so make it about the sequence rather than the clock. After a night shift: sunglasses on the way home to limit morning light, a light meal, the same three phases compressed into 30 to 40 minutes, then a dark room. This is where a sleep mask earns its place, because daylight through curtains is the biggest obstacle to daytime sleep. Keep the same routine before every sleep, day or night, so the signal survives the rota.

If you have young children

Your evening is not yours until the children are down, and by then you may have an hour before you need to be asleep. Fold phase one into their bedtime (lights low across the house, your list written while they have their story) so that when they are asleep you can go straight to phase two. Accept that phase three will often be ten minutes. Ten minutes nightly is enough.

If you finish work late

Late finishers (hospitality, evening classes, a second job, a habit of working until 11) come home switched on. The mistake is going straight from the last task to bed. Give phase one a hard start when you walk in: coat off, list written, phone charging in the kitchen. Then a shower, dim light, and something slow. The routine may end at 1am; what matters is that it happens, and that you get up at a consistent time.

If you live with someone on a different schedule

Agree on the shared parts (lights down after a certain hour, no television in the bedroom) and keep the rest personal. A reading light that does not spill and a sleep mask for whoever goes to bed first remove most of the friction. If one of you gets up at 5, the other one's routine is not the problem; the early alarm is, and a mask and earplugs are the practical answer.

Small comforts that help some people

A routine does not need equipment, but two things genuinely help some people settle.

A sleep mask makes phase three portable: it gives you darkness in a shared room, a bright flat or a hotel. And some people find the even pressure of a weighted blanket calming at the end of the day; the evidence for weighted blankets and sleep is limited and mixed, but the effect on feeling settled is something many people describe. If you are curious, our weighted blankets guide is honest about what they can and cannot do.

If darkness or gentle pressure helps you settle

Neither is essential to a routine, but for people who share a room or who find even pressure calming, they can make the last phase easier.

Why consistency matters more than perfection

The reason routines work is not any single ingredient. It is that your body learns to predict sleep from a pattern, and prediction needs repetition. A modest routine done most nights produces a stronger signal than an elaborate one done when you have the energy.

This connects to the single most important sleep habit of all, which is getting up at about the same time every day. The wake time sets the clock; the evening routine simply helps you meet it. If your bedtime is all over the place, a fixed wake time will do more than any evening ritual, and our guide to a consistent sleep schedule explains why.

Give a new routine two to three weeks before judging it. The first few nights often feel artificial. That is normal, and it passes.

What to leave out

The routines people abandon are usually the ones with too much in them. A few things are worth dropping on purpose.

Anything you resent. If journaling feels like a chore, write a three-line list. If meditation makes you tense, read. The routine should feel like a relief, not a second shift.

Anything that measures sleep. Sleep trackers and apps can be interesting, but checking a score in the morning turns sleep into a performance, and for people who already lie awake that tends to make things worse. If you use one, look at it weekly rather than daily.

And anything with a claim attached. Teas, sprays, supplements and gadgets marketed as sleep aids rarely have strong evidence behind them. Melatonin is prescription-only in the UK, and a pharmacist is the right person to ask about anything else. A routine works because it is repeated, not because of what is in it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a bedtime routine be?

Around an hour is a good target, but 20 minutes done nightly beats 90 minutes done twice a week. The value comes from repetition. If an hour is unrealistic, keep the last 20 minutes (dim light, no screens, one calming activity) and let the rest be flexible.

Do I need a bedtime routine if I sleep fine?

Not necessarily. If you fall asleep easily and wake refreshed, you already have one, even if it is informal. Routines matter most for people who lie awake, whose evenings vary a lot, or whose work keeps them switched on late.

What if my routine gets interrupted?

Do the shortest version and move on. A routine is a signal, not a spell. Missing it on a Friday does not undo the week, and worrying about it will do more harm than the missed steps.
