Bedtime Relaxation Techniques: Which Ones Work and How to Choose

Relaxation techniques give a wired body and a busy mind a simple job to do instead of trying to force sleep. This guide compares the main methods, says what the evidence supports, and helps you pick one to try tonight.

The bedtime relaxation techniques with the best track record are slow breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, the body scan, and meditation or mindfulness practice, with guided imagery, yoga nidra, gentle stretching, steady sound, a warm bath and reading as useful supporting habits. None of them makes you sleep. What they do is lower the physical and mental arousal that stops sleep arriving on its own.

If you want a decision now: if your body feels wound up, start with progressive muscle relaxation. If your mind is racing but your body is fine, start with a body scan or breath counting. If you are simply not sleepy, no technique will fix that tonight, and the answer is in your routine rather than in bed.

What relaxation techniques do, and what they cannot do

Sleep needs two things: enough sleep pressure (you have been awake long enough) and low enough arousal (your body and mind are not on alert). Relaxation techniques work on the second. They cannot supply the first.

That matters, because people often use relaxation to try to overpower a body that is not ready for sleep, and then conclude that "nothing works". If you regularly lie awake calm but alert, the fix is usually a fixed wake time and a later bedtime, and our guide to what to do when you cannot sleep covers that side.

Where relaxation earns its place is the very common situation of being tired but wired: physically tense, mentally busy, and getting more so the harder you try to drop off. Relaxation training is a long-standing component of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which the NHS and the European insomnia guideline recommend as the first-line treatment. It is well established as part of that package, and less effective alone.

In short: Relaxation lowers arousal; it does not create sleepiness. Use it to stop fighting for sleep, not as a way to force it.

A quick chooser

  • Heart going, shoulders tight, jaw clenched: progressive muscle relaxation, then slow breathing.
  • Thoughts racing, body fine: body scan or breath counting. If the thoughts are worries rather than noise, a worry list before bed helps more than any technique in bed.
  • Anxious about sleep itself, checking the clock: mindfulness, which works on the effort rather than the symptom.
  • Tired but restless, want something physical: ten minutes of gentle stretches, then breathing.
  • Too tired to do anything active: guided relaxation or yoga nidra, following a voice.
  • Woken at 3am: extended-exhale breathing or a short body scan, without checking the time.
  • Bedroom noisy or you are a light sleeper: steady sound alongside whichever technique you prefer.

Whatever you pick, use it every night for two weeks. Familiar techniques work better than novel ones, because the body learns to associate them with winding down.

The techniques compared

Each entry gives the method in a sentence, why it works, who it suits and what to watch for, with a link to the full step-by-step guide.

Breathing exercises

What it is: slowing the breath, with a longer out-breath than in-breath, for five to ten minutes. Why it works: a long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve, the main channel of the body's rest-and-digest system, and slows the heart. Best for: almost everyone as a starting point; the quickest technique to learn. Watch out for: dizziness from over-breathing or long holds; if that happens, drop the holds and use shorter counts.

Our guide to breathing exercises for sleep walks through five patterns. Two deserve their own mention. 4-7-8 breathing is the most famous, with a long hold; it suits people who like a fixed ritual, though the specific numbers have little research behind them. Box breathing, four counts on each side, gives the mind more structure and is popular for daytime stress as well.

Progressive muscle relaxation

What it is: tensing each muscle group for five seconds, releasing, and noticing the contrast, from the feet to the face. Why it works: it teaches you what "relaxed" feels like and gives a wound-up body something active to do. Best for: physical tension, and minds that need more than the breath to hold onto. Watch out for: pain or injury; never tense anything that hurts, and adapt or skip those regions.

This is the most studied relaxation method in insomnia treatment. The full script, with timings and adaptations, is in our guide to progressive muscle relaxation for sleep.

Body scan

What it is: moving attention slowly through the body, region by region, noticing whatever is there without trying to change it. Why it works: it occupies the mind with an orderly, undemanding path, and relaxation follows as a side effect. Best for: busy minds, and anyone who cannot tense muscles safely. Watch out for: turning it into a relaxation test; the aim is noticing, not fixing.

The body scan for sleep guide has a full script and covers what to do when you fall asleep halfway through (nothing; that is the point).

Meditation and mindfulness

What it is: resting attention on the breath, the body or sounds, and returning gently each time the mind wanders. Mindfulness adds a change of attitude: noticing thoughts and the urge to sleep without acting on them. Why it works: it takes the effort out of trying to sleep, which is often the main thing keeping people awake. Best for: sleep anxiety, clock-watching, and anyone who has "tried everything". Watch out for: a minority find focused attention energising; widen the focus or practise earlier in the evening.

Mindfulness-based programmes show modest benefits for sleep quality in some trials, mostly over several weeks of practice. Our beginner's guide to bedtime meditation gives three simple practices, and mindfulness for sleep explains the acceptance approach in depth.

Guided relaxation and imagery

The widely shared military sleep method is essentially a compressed version of the two techniques below: a quick muscle release followed by a fixed image.

What it is: following a recorded voice through a body scan, a breathing sequence, a peaceful scene or a slow story. Why it works: the voice does the remembering and the pacing, so you need only listen. Best for: beginners, very tired people, and anyone whose mind will not stay with a silent practice. Watch out for: dependence on a phone at bedtime, and voices or lengths that irritate rather than soothe.

Our guide to guided relaxation for sleep covers the types, choosing a voice and length, and free sources.

Yoga nidra

What it is: a guided, lying-down practice from the yoga tradition that rotates attention around the body and through breath and imagery, usually twenty to forty minutes. It is the basis of what some people now call "NSDR" (non-sleep deep rest). Why it works: it is a structured deep-rest practice with a fixed sequence, which many find easier than open meditation. Best for: people who want a longer, fully guided practice. Watch out for: the evidence is limited, though promising for relaxation; treat claims about replacing sleep with scepticism.

More in our guide to yoga nidra for sleep.

Gentle stretches

What it is: six to eight slow, easy stretches in dim light in the half hour before bed. Why it works: it releases the physical stiffness of a day at a desk and pairs naturally with slow breathing. Best for: restless legs, stiff backs and shoulders, and people who need to move a little before they can be still. Watch out for: anything that hurts, bouncing, or making it a workout; keep it slow, and be careful with existing back or joint problems.

The routine is in our guide to gentle bedtime stretches.

Steady sound

Rain, brown noise, a fan or a white noise machine are not relaxation techniques in themselves, but they support them. Steady sound masks the car doors and creaking pipes that keep a light sleeper on alert, and it gives a restless mind something neutral to rest on. Sound sits alongside a breathing exercise or body scan rather than replacing it.

A warm bath or shower

A warm bath an hour or two before bed is one of the better-supported bedtime habits. A 2019 systematic review by Haghayegh and colleagues in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that warm baths or showers of around 40 to 42°C, taken one to two hours before bed, were associated with falling asleep faster. The likely mechanism is that warming the skin helps the body shed core heat afterwards, and a falling core temperature is one of the signals for sleep. Ten minutes is enough; it does not need to be a long soak.

Reading

Reading a physical book in low light is a way of relaxing rather than a technique, and its value is mainly that it displaces the phone and gives the mind something absorbing but undemanding. Choose something you have read before or that is gently paced. If you find yourself reading to stay awake for the next chapter, it has stopped doing its job.

Habits that make any technique work better

Relaxation techniques struggle against a bright room, a late coffee or a phone on the pillow. Before you blame the technique, check the basics: lights dimmed for an hour before bed, screens away, bedroom cool and dark, a fixed wake time seven days a week. A relaxation technique is the last step of a routine, not a substitute for one.

Two product types genuinely help some people here. If you find gentle, even pressure calming, a weighted blanket can make a body scan or breathing practice feel more settled; the evidence is limited and it is a preference, not a treatment, as our weighted blankets guide explains. If you follow guided audio in bed, comfortable sleep headphones make it practical without disturbing a partner; see our guide to the best sleep headphones.

Supporting kit for relaxation in bed

Neither is necessary, but pressure and comfortable audio each make certain techniques easier for some people.

When to get more help

If you have tried a technique consistently for a few weeks and still lie awake most nights, or you are dreading bed, that is a sign the problem is bigger than arousal. Persistent insomnia responds well to CBT-I, which is available through the NHS in some areas and through digital programmes. Our guide to when to see a GP about sleep sets out what to look for and what to ask.

Tonight's version

Pick one from the chooser above. Do the basics first: lights down, phone away. Then, in bed, give the technique ten minutes with no expectation of sleep. If you drift off, good. If not, lie quietly for a while; if you become frustrated, get up briefly, sit in dim light, and come back when sleepy. Repeat tomorrow. The technique gets stronger with repetition, and so does the signal to your body that the day is over.

Frequently asked questions

How long before bed should I do a relaxation technique?

Most work best in bed with the lights off, as the final step of the evening. Stretches and a warm bath belong earlier, in the hour before bed. Whatever you choose, do the screens-off, lights-down part of the routine first, so the technique is not fighting a bright room.

Which relaxation technique works fastest?

Slow breathing with a long out-breath changes the body's state within a few minutes and is the quickest to learn. But 'fastest' is the wrong measure for sleep: the technique that helps most is the one you can do calmly every night without checking whether it has worked yet.

Can relaxation techniques replace treatment for insomnia?

No. Relaxation training is one component of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the recommended first-line treatment, and it works best alongside a regular wake time and getting up when you are lying awake for long stretches. If poor sleep has lasted more than a few weeks, speak to a GP.
