Mindfulness for Sleep: Letting Go of the Effort to Fall Asleep

The harder you try to sleep, the further it moves away. Mindfulness for sleep works on that struggle itself: noticing thoughts and urges without acting on them, and letting sleep arrive rather than chasing it. Here is how, with practices for tonight.

By the Quiet Mind Sleep editorial team · Published 25 September 2026 · 6 min read

Mindfulness for sleep is not another technique to make you drop off. It is a change of stance. Instead of trying to control sleep, you practise noticing what is actually happening, thoughts, sensations, the urge to check the clock, and letting it be there without acting on it. Sleep is not something you can do; it is something that happens when you stop doing.

That sounds passive, and in a sense it is. But for people who have tried every breathing pattern and pillow spray and still lie there monitoring for sleep, dropping the effort is often the thing that finally helps. This guide explains the idea, gives three short practices, and sets out what the research honestly shows.

Acceptance versus control

Most of what keeps people awake at night is effort. You notice you are still awake, you calculate how many hours are left, you try harder to relax, you check whether it is working. Each step raises alertness a little more. Sleep researchers call this hyperarousal: a body and mind kept on alert by the very attempt to switch them off.

The control approach says: find the right technique and apply it until sleep happens. It works for some people, some of the time. Its weakness is that when it fails, it feeds the struggle.

The acceptance approach says: you cannot make sleep happen, so stop trying. Lie in bed awake if that is what is happening. Notice the thoughts, notice the frustration, notice the wish to be asleep, and let all of it be present without fighting it. Paradoxically, when the effort drops, arousal drops with it, and sleep has room to arrive.

This is the core of mindfulness-based approaches to insomnia, and it is why they overlap so closely with the treatment of sleep anxiety, where worry about sleep is itself the problem.

In short: You cannot force sleep, but you can stop fighting for it. Mindfulness is the practice of noticing the fight and setting it down.

Noticing thoughts without following them

The central skill is simple to describe and takes practice to do. A thought arrives: "I have to be up at six." Ordinarily you follow it: "that means five hours if I fall asleep now, which I won't, and tomorrow is the presentation..." and you are off.

Mindfulness means noticing the thought as a thought. "There is the thought about six o'clock." Then letting it pass, the way you would let a car pass on the road outside, and returning attention to something steady: the breath, the weight of the body, the sounds in the room.

You will follow thoughts anyway, dozens of times a night. The practice is not never following them. It is noticing, a little sooner each time, that you have, and coming back without telling yourself off.

Three short practices

Do these in bed, lights off, with no goal of sleep. If sleep comes, fine. If it does not, you have spent the time resting rather than struggling, which is itself worthwhile.

1. Naming what is here (five minutes)

  1. Lie still and take three unhurried breaths.
  2. Ask silently, "what is here right now?" and answer with a single word: "thinking", "tightness", "warm", "worry", "sound".
  3. Whatever the answer, let it stay. Do not try to change it. After a few seconds, ask again.
  4. Continue at a lazy pace. If the answer is "trying to sleep", name that too, and let it be there like everything else.

2. The anchor and the wandering (ten minutes)

  1. Choose an anchor: the breath at the belly, or the feeling of the body on the mattress.
  2. Rest attention on it. Not hard focus, only a light touch.
  3. When you notice you have wandered, note where you went ("planning", "remembering", "worrying"), and come back to the anchor.
  4. Treat each return as the point of the exercise. Forty returns is forty repetitions, not forty failures.

A longer version of this, with the attention moving through the body region by region, is the body scan, the standard first practice in mindfulness courses.

3. Welcoming wakefulness (any length)

This is the practice for the worst nights, when frustration is building.

  1. Say silently, "I am awake, and that is what is happening right now."
  2. Notice the urge to fix it, and the story about tomorrow. Name them: "urge", "story".
  3. Give yourself permission to rest without sleeping. Resting awake in a dark room is not nothing; the body still benefits.
  4. Return to the anchor of your choice, and let the night be whatever length it is.

If you have been awake for what feels like a long time and rest has turned into stewing, the standard advice still applies: get up, sit somewhere dim, and come back when sleepy. Our guide on whether to get up when you cannot sleep explains why that helps.

Mindfulness in the day

Mindfulness practised only at bedtime tends to become one more thing you are doing to get to sleep. It works better when it is also part of the day. Ten minutes in the morning, or a few mindful breaths while the kettle boils, builds the skill of noticing and returning so that it is available at night without effort.

You do not need to sit cross-legged or use an app. Full attention on the walk to the station, or on the first sip of a cup of tea, is mindfulness. The NHS mindfulness page describes the approach and points to structured courses.

What the evidence says

Mindfulness for sleep has a reasonable and growing evidence base, though it is not as strong as the evidence for cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

A 2014 randomised controlled trial by Ong and colleagues, published in Sleep, compared mindfulness-based programmes with a self-monitoring control in adults with chronic insomnia and found meaningful reductions in wakefulness and insomnia severity, sustained at follow-up. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Rusch and colleagues concluded that mindfulness meditation modestly improves sleep quality compared with non-specific controls.

The honest summary is that mindfulness-based programmes show modest benefits in some trials, mostly after several weeks of structured practice rather than a single night. It sits well alongside CBT-I and is sometimes combined with it. It is not a substitute for medical advice if insomnia is severe or long-standing; our guide to when to see a GP about sleep covers that.

Where to find structured help

The NHS recommends mindfulness as one approach to mental wellbeing, and the NHS mindfulness page explains what it involves and how to find a course. Eight-week programmes such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy are available in many areas, sometimes through NHS talking therapies services, and there are free guided practices from university mindfulness centres. Our beginner's guide to bedtime meditation covers guided versus silent practice and free options.

The short version

Tonight, when you notice you are trying to sleep, name it: "trying". Let it be there. Rest attention lightly on the breath or the body, and when you drift into planning, come back. Do not check whether it is working. You are not doing this to sleep; you are doing it to stop fighting.
