4-7-8 Breathing for Sleep: How to Do It and What to Expect

Breathe in for four, hold for seven, out for eight, four times over. Here is exactly how to do 4-7-8 breathing, what it can realistically do for sleep, and who should use a gentler version.

By the Quiet Mind Sleep editorial team · Published 25 September 2026 · 6 min read

  2. Where 4-7-8 breathing comes from

4-7-8 breathing is a slow breathing pattern: you breathe in for a count of four, hold for a count of seven and breathe out for a count of eight. Done a few times over, it slows the heart and steadies the body, which for many people makes it easier to drift towards sleep.

It will not knock you out in sixty seconds, whatever the videos say. What it can do is calm a wound-up body and give a busy mind something to count. Here are the steps, followed by where the technique comes from, what the evidence honestly shows, and when a version without the long hold is a better fit.

How to do 4-7-8 breathing

Practise sitting up the first couple of times so you can learn the counts without worrying about sleep. After that, do it lying in bed with the lights off.

  1. Place your tongue. Rest the tip of your tongue on the ridge just behind your top front teeth and keep it there. It is an odd instruction, borrowed from yogic breathing, and it stops you breathing out too fast.
  2. Empty your lungs. Breathe out completely through your mouth, letting the air escape with a soft whoosh around your tongue.
  3. Breathe in for four. Close your mouth and breathe in quietly through your nose while you count silently to four. Let the belly rise rather than the chest.
  4. Hold for seven. Keep the breath in, without clamping down. The throat stays soft; you are simply pausing.
  5. Breathe out for eight. Open your mouth and let the air out slowly, with the same soft sound (the sound is optional; breathing out through the nose works too), while you count to eight. Aim for the exhale to last the whole count rather than escaping in the first two seconds.
  6. Repeat. That is one cycle. Do four cycles, building to eight once it feels easy. Then stop counting and let your breath settle at whatever slow pace it has found.

Timing: the counts are ratios, not seconds. If holding for seven full seconds is a strain, count faster so the whole cycle takes ten seconds or so, and slow down as it becomes familiar. What matters is that the out-breath is twice as long as the in-breath.

What to notice: a slight heaviness in the limbs after the second or third cycle, and the heart rate settling. If your mind wanders, which it will, notice where it went and pick up the count from wherever you are. Losing count is not a failure; returning is the practice.

If you feel light-headed or tingly, stop and breathe normally. This is the most common problem with 4-7-8 and it comes from taking in more air than you need or holding for too long. Next time, take a smaller in-breath and shorten the hold.

Where 4-7-8 breathing comes from

The technique was popularised by Andrew Weil, an American doctor and writer on integrative medicine, who has described it as a "natural tranquiliser for the nervous system". He drew it from pranayama, the breath-control practices of yoga, where ratio breathing with holds has been used for centuries as a way to steady the mind.

The numbers themselves are not magic; Weil's own advice is that the ratio is what counts and that beginners should speed the count up rather than struggle. 4-7-8 became well known because it is easy to remember, which is also why it appears in almost every list of breathing exercises for sleep.

What the evidence says

Slow breathing in general is well studied. A 2018 systematic review by Zaccaro and colleagues found that breathing at around six breaths a minute is consistently linked with increased heart-rate variability, lower reported anxiety and greater feelings of relaxation. Long, slow exhales stimulate the vagus nerve, the main channel of the body's "rest and digest" system, and that is a plausible mechanism for feeling calmer.

4-7-8 specifically has been studied very little. There are no large, well-controlled trials showing that this pattern helps people fall asleep faster than any other slow breathing pattern, or than simply lying quietly. Claims that it works "in a minute" or is "the most powerful" technique have no research behind them.

So the sensible way to think about 4-7-8 is as one convenient way to do slow breathing, with the bonus that the fixed structure gives your mind a task. If it suits you, that is reason enough. If not, you lose nothing by using a simpler pattern.

In short: Slow breathing with a long exhale has good evidence for calming the body. The specific 4-7-8 numbers do not, and that is fine. Use whichever slow pattern you can do comfortably every night.

Tips for making it work at bedtime

  • Do it every evening, not only on bad nights. Relaxation techniques work better once the body has learned to associate them with winding down. A fortnight of consistent practice is a fair trial.
  • Do it after the rest of your routine, not instead of it. Breathing cannot overcome a bright room and a phone in your hand.
  • Do not check whether it is working. Monitoring for sleep keeps you awake. Finish the cycles, let go of the count and simply lie there.
  • Pair it with something for the mind if you need to. If thoughts keep interrupting, a technique like cognitive shuffling can follow on naturally once the breathing has settled the body.
  • Use it in the night as well. If you wake at 3am, four quiet cycles are a good first response. If you are still awake long after, see our guide on what to do when you cannot get back to sleep.

When 4-7-8 is not the right choice

The long hold is what sets 4-7-8 apart, and it is also the part that does not suit everyone.

  • If breath-holding makes you anxious. Some people with health anxiety or panic find the hold unsettling: attention on a held breath can start the very spiral you were trying to avoid. Skip the hold and use a four-in, six-out pattern. Our guide to anxiety at night explains why the mind does this.
  • If you get dizzy easily. Shorten everything: three in, four hold, five out, or drop the hold.
  • If you are pregnant, have a heart or lung condition, or have been told to avoid breath-holding, ask a GP, midwife or physiotherapist before adding holds. Slow breathing without holds is generally fine.
  • If you prefer equal counts. Some people simply find box breathing, with its four equal sides, easier to keep track of when tired.

None of this makes 4-7-8 risky for most people. It means the hold is optional, and a version without it is still a good slow breathing exercise.

Where to go next

If 4-7-8 leaves you calmer but still awake, the next step is usually a technique that works on the body, such as progressive muscle relaxation, or a look at the bigger picture: a regular wake time, enough sleep pressure, a dark cool room. The Relaxation hub compares the main methods so you can choose one to learn properly.

Tonight: tongue behind the teeth, empty the lungs, in for four, hold for seven, out for eight. Four times. Then let the breath go and see what happens.
