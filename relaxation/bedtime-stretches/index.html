Gentle Bedtime Stretches to Help You Unwind

A few slow, easy stretches in dim light release the stiffness of a day at a desk and signal to the body that it is winding down. Here are seven to do in the half hour before bed, with the safety notes that matter.

By the Quiet Mind Sleep editorial team · Published 25 September 2026 · 6 min read

  2. The routine
  3. Pairing stretches with breathing

Bedtime stretches work best when they are slow, gentle and slightly boring. The aim is not flexibility or fitness. It is to release the stiffness of a day of sitting, slow the breath, and give your body ten quiet minutes of doing one thing at a time before bed.

Below are seven stretches described in words, so you can do them in a dim room without a screen. Each is held for thirty seconds to a minute with slow breathing; the routine takes about ten minutes. Safety notes come first, because a stretch that hurts is worse than none.

Before you start: the rules

  • Nothing should hurt. A gentle pull is the aim. Sharp pain, tingling or numbness means stop.
  • No bouncing. Move into each stretch slowly, hold still, and come out slowly.
  • Breathe throughout. Never hold your breath in a stretch. The out-breath is when you let the stretch deepen slightly, if it wants to.
  • Keep it dim. A bedside lamp or the landing light, not the main bedroom light. Bright light in the half hour before bed works against you; our guide to bedroom lighting for sleep explains why.
  • Keep it slow. If you find yourself speeding up or adding more, you have drifted into exercise, which raises heart rate and temperature late in the evening; the relationship between exercise and sleep depends heavily on timing and intensity.
  • If you have a back, neck, hip or knee problem, osteoporosis, or you are pregnant, skip anything below that involves that area or feels wrong, and check with a physiotherapist or GP about what is suitable.

The routine

Do these in order, on the carpet or on the bed, in loose clothing. Where a stretch is one-sided, do both sides.

1. Neck: ear towards shoulder

Sit comfortably, on the edge of the bed or cross-legged. Let your right ear drift down towards your right shoulder, keeping the left shoulder low. Do not pull with your hand; the weight of the head is enough. Breathe slowly for thirty seconds, return to centre and repeat on the left.

Notice: the long line down the side of the neck, and how much the shoulders want to creep up. Let them drop.

2. Shoulder rolls and a chest opener

Still sitting, roll both shoulders slowly backwards in large circles five times, then forwards five times. Then clasp your hands behind your back, or hold a towel between them, and gently draw the hands down and away so the chest opens. Hold for thirty seconds, breathing into the front of the chest.

Notice: the space opening across the collarbones after a day of leaning towards a screen.

3. Seated forward fold

Sit on the floor or bed with legs out in front, knees soft. Breathe in and lengthen the spine; breathe out and lean forward from the hips, hands resting on the shins, ankles or feet, wherever they reach without strain. Let the head hang. Hold for forty-five seconds to a minute.

Safety: if you have lower back problems or osteoporosis, keep the back long rather than rounding, bend only a little, or skip this one. A rolled towel under the knees makes it gentler.

4. Cat and cow

Come onto hands and knees, wrists under shoulders, knees under hips. Breathe in and let the belly drop while the chest lifts (cow). Breathe out and round the back towards the ceiling, tucking the chin (cat). Move slowly, one shape per breath, for eight to ten breaths.

Notice: the spine loosening a vertebra at a time. Keep the movement small if the lower back is sensitive.

5. Child's pose

From hands and knees, take the knees wide, bring the big toes together, and sit the hips back towards the heels. Stretch the arms forward along the floor and rest the forehead down. Breathe slowly into the back of the ribs for a minute or so.

Safety: if the knees complain, put a folded pillow between the hips and heels. If you are pregnant, take the knees wider or leave this one out.

6. Knees to chest, then a gentle twist

Lie on your back. Draw both knees in towards the chest and hold them loosely, rocking slightly side to side if that feels good, for thirty seconds.

Then keep the knees bent, feet flat, and let both knees drop slowly to the right while the shoulders stay on the floor. Arms out to the sides or hands resting on the belly. Hold for forty-five seconds, breathing into the left side of the back, then bring the knees to centre and repeat to the left.

Safety: keep the twist small and keep both shoulders down. Skip it if you have a disc problem, a recent back injury or are in later pregnancy.

7. Legs up the wall, or a supported figure-four

Lie on your back with your hips close to a wall and rest your legs up it, or rest the calves on a chair if a wall is awkward. Arms by your sides, palms up. Stay for two to three minutes, breathing slowly. This is where the routine hands over to rest: heavy legs, slow breath, nothing left to do.

If your legs feel restless rather than heavy, try a figure-four instead: on your back, knees bent, cross the right ankle over the left knee and draw the left thigh gently towards you until you feel the stretch in the right hip. Forty-five seconds, then swap.

Safety: legs up the wall is not advised if you have uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma, or are in later pregnancy; use the calves-on-a-chair version or leave it out.

Pairing stretches with breathing

The stretches and the breath do more together than apart. Breathe in to prepare, breathe out to move into the stretch, then breathe slowly for the hold with the out-breath a little longer than the in-breath. That pattern, four in and six out, is the foundation of every breathing exercise for sleep, and the stretches give you ten minutes of practising it without thinking.

When you finish, lie still for a minute, notice the difference, and go straight to bed rather than checking your phone. If tension lingers when the light goes off, the muscle relaxation script picks up where the stretches leave off.

Where stretches fit in the evening

Stretching is a wind-down activity, not a bed activity. It belongs in the last half hour before you get in, after screens are off and lights are down, as part of a bedtime routine that runs in the same order most nights. Done consistently, the routine itself becomes the cue.

The evidence for stretching specifically as a sleep aid is thin; no large trials show that ten minutes of stretching shortens the time it takes to fall asleep. What is well supported is a calm, dim, repeated wind-down and slow breathing, and stretches are a pleasant way to deliver both. The Relaxation hub compares them with the techniques you can use once you are in bed.

Tonight's version

Lamp on, main light off. Neck, shoulders, forward fold, cat and cow, child's pose, knees and twist, legs up the wall: thirty seconds to a minute each, breathing out to move and breathing slowly to hold. Nothing should hurt. Then lights off and into bed while the body is still heavy.
