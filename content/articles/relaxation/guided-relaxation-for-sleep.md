Guided relaxation for sleep means lying in bed and following a recorded voice that tells you, slowly, where to put your attention: on your breath, on each part of your body, on an imagined walk along a quiet beach. You do not have to remember a script, keep count or decide what comes next. The voice does all of that. You only listen.

That is why it works so well for tired people. Every silent relaxation technique asks you to manage your own attention, and at 11pm after a long day that is often the one thing you cannot do. Guided audio outsources the task. When your mind drifts, the voice pulls it back within a few seconds, without any effort on your part.

This guide covers the main types, how to choose a voice and a length, where to find free recordings, and how to listen in bed without discomfort or disturbing anyone else.

## What guided audio actually does

Two things keep people awake once the lights are off: a body that is still on alert and a mind with nothing better to do than think. Relaxation techniques address both by giving the body a slow rhythm and the mind a simple task. Guided audio does the same, with one difference: the task is delivered to you, so the mind has somewhere to rest even when it is too tired to direct itself.

A good recording also sets the pace. Left alone, most of us rush a body scan or count our breaths too fast; a voice that pauses for ten seconds between instructions slows you down.

What guided audio cannot do is make you sleepy if you are not. It reduces arousal; it does not create sleep pressure. If you are calm but wide awake at the end of every recording, the issue is timing rather than technique.

> **In short:** Guided relaxation takes the attention job off your hands. That is its whole advantage, and for a tired mind it is a big one.

## The main types

**Guided body scan.** The voice moves your attention slowly through the body from the feet upwards, region by region. This is the most common type and a good first choice. The silent version, with a script, is in our guide to the [body scan for sleep](@relaxation/body-scan-for-sleep).

**Guided breathing.** Paced breathing tracks that simply say "in" and "out", or count for you. Short, and useful when you want something minimal.

**Guided progressive muscle relaxation.** Tense-and-release instructions for each muscle group. Suits physical tension. The full method is in our [progressive muscle relaxation script](@relaxation/progressive-muscle-relaxation-for-sleep).

**Guided imagery.** The voice describes a calm scene in detail, a woodland path, a warm garden, and invites you to notice what you would see, hear and feel. Good for people who think in pictures and find body-based practices dull. Less good if you are the kind of person who argues with the details.

**Yoga nidra.** A structured, lying-down practice of twenty to forty minutes that rotates attention around the body and through breath and imagery. Longer and more formal than a body scan. See our guide to [yoga nidra for sleep](@relaxation/yoga-nidra-for-sleep).

**Sleep stories and "talk-downs".** A slow, deliberately uneventful story or a soft monologue designed to be boring in a pleasant way. These give the mind a gentle thread to follow without asking anything of it, and many people find them the easiest thing to fall asleep to.

## Choosing a voice and a length

**Voice.** This is personal, and it matters more than the content. A voice you find grating will keep you awake no matter how well written the script. Try two or three recordings and keep the one you stop noticing. Slower is better; a voice that fills every second leaves no room to drift.

**Length.** For bedtime, ten to twenty minutes is the sweet spot. Shorter recordings end before you have settled; longer ones are fine if you fall asleep during them, but a forty-minute track you keep restarting becomes a project. For 3am wakings, five to ten minutes is plenty.

**Ending.** Choose recordings that fade out or end silently. A cheerful "and now you feel refreshed and alert" is for daytime practice, not for bed.

**Music and sound.** Some people like a soft background; others find it distracting. Recordings with a steady sound bed can double as noise masking, which our guide to [calming sounds for anxiety at night](@sleep-sounds/calming-sounds-for-anxiety-at-night) discusses.

## Free sources

You do not need a subscription. Worth knowing about:

- **NHS Every Mind Matters** has short, free audio and video relaxation exercises, including breathing and body-focused practices, alongside its sleep advice.
- **NHS mindfulness resources** point to free guided practices and to structured courses.
- **University mindfulness centres** in the UK and abroad publish free guided body scans and sitting practices of various lengths.
- **Public libraries** often provide free access to audio apps with relaxation and mindfulness content.
- **Meditation and sleep apps** usually have a free tier with a handful of sleep recordings. They are convenient, but no app is necessary, and we do not endorse any particular one.

We intend to add our own free recordings to this page in future. Until then, the sources above are a good place to start.

Once you have found a recording you like, stop browsing; scrolling for the perfect track at bedtime is screen time by another name.

## Listening comfortably in bed

Guided relaxation has one practical snag: it involves a device and a sound source in a room where you are trying to wind down.

**Keep the phone dim and out of reach.** Set the recording going, turn the screen off, and put the phone face down on the far side of the bedside table. Use a sleep timer or a recording that ends by itself so you are not reaching for it afterwards.

**Decide how you will hear it.** A speaker is fine if you sleep alone and the volume is low. If you share a bed, or you are a side sleeper, ordinary earbuds press into the ear and wired ones tangle. Headband headphones, which sit flat in a soft fabric band, and small sleep earbuds designed to be lain on are the two usual answers; our guide to the [best sleep headphones](@sleep-products/best-sleep-headphones-uk) explains the trade-offs, and there is a shorter [comparison of headband, earbud and bone-conduction designs](@sleep-products/types-of-sleep-headphones) if you just want the differences.

**Keep the volume low.** Quiet enough that you have to settle to hear it. A recording that ends silently is kinder than one that loops all night.

## Moving from guided to silent

Guided audio is a good place to start and a perfectly good place to stay. But it helps to learn one practice well enough to do it without a recording, so it is available at 3am without a phone. Alternate guided and silent nights with the same practice for a fortnight and notice which you prefer.

## Tonight's version

Pick a ten- to twenty-minute guided body scan from a free source. Lights off, phone face down, volume low. Lie in your normal position and let the voice do the work. If you drift off before the end, that is the point. If you reach the end awake, lie quietly for a while; do not restart it or go looking for a better one.
