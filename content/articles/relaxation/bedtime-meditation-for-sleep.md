Bedtime meditation means lying in bed and resting your attention on one simple thing, usually the breath or the body, and returning to it gently each time your mind drifts off. That is all. It is not emptying the mind, and it is not a trick that makes sleep arrive on schedule.

What it offers is a way out of the trap that keeps many people awake: trying hard to sleep, checking whether it is working, and getting more alert with every check. Meditation gives your attention somewhere undemanding to rest, so that sleep can turn up on its own.

Below are three beginner practices you can do tonight, guidance on guided versus silent versions, a plain account of the evidence, and what to do if meditating in bed makes you more awake rather than less.

## What bedtime meditation is and is not

**It is:** a few minutes of resting attention on something steady, noticing when the mind wanders, and coming back without judgement. The coming back is the practice. If you have to do it forty times, you have meditated forty times.

**It is not:** stopping thoughts, achieving a blank mind, or relaxing on command. Thoughts will keep coming. The change is in how you relate to them: as passing weather rather than as things you have to deal with now. Our guide to [mindfulness for sleep](@relaxation/mindfulness-for-sleep) goes deeper into that shift.

**It is also not a sleep technique in the strict sense.** If you use it while quietly watching for sleep, it becomes another form of effort. The most useful mindset is: "I am going to rest attention on my breath for a while. Sleep may or may not come. Either is fine."

> **In short:** Meditation for sleep works by taking the pressure off, not by adding a new job. Do it as if sleep were optional.

## Three simple practices

Each takes five to fifteen minutes. Choose one and use it every night for a couple of weeks before deciding whether it suits you. Do them lying in bed, lights off, in your usual sleeping position.

### 1. Following the breath

The most basic practice, and the one to start with.

1. Settle into a comfortable position and let your eyes close.
2. Take two slower breaths, then let the breath return to normal. You are not controlling it, only watching it.
3. Choose one place to feel the breath: the air at the nostrils, the rise and fall of the belly, or the chest. Stay with that one place.
4. Follow each in-breath from beginning to end, and each out-breath from beginning to end. Notice the small pause before the next one.
5. When you realise your mind has wandered, which might be within three breaths, note "thinking" silently and return to the next breath. No frustration required; this is the exercise working as designed.
6. Continue for as long as you like. If you drift off, that is a fine ending.

**What to notice:** the breath slowing by itself after a few minutes, and the gaps between thoughts getting slightly longer.

### 2. A short body scan

Better for people whose minds are too busy to stay with something as small as the breath.

1. Bring attention to your feet. Notice whatever is there: warmth, the weight of the duvet, tingling, or nothing at all. Nothing needs to change.
2. After twenty or thirty seconds, move to the lower legs, then the knees, thighs, hips, belly, chest, hands, arms, shoulders, neck, face and head, spending a little time on each.
3. If you find tension, notice it and move on. You are not trying to relax each part, only to visit it.
4. Finish by sensing the whole body at once, lying heavy on the bed.

This practice has a full script, with pacing and what to do if you fall asleep halfway, in our guide to the [body scan for sleep](@relaxation/body-scan-for-sleep).

### 3. Labelling sounds and thoughts

For nights when both body and breath feel like too much effort, this practice simply lets everything be there.

1. Lie still and listen. Notice each sound as it arrives: a car outside, the boiler, your own breath. Silently label each one "sound" and let it pass.
2. When a thought arrives instead, label it "thought" and let it pass in the same way. Do not follow it, argue with it, or push it away.
3. When an emotion or a body sensation shows up, label that too: "worry", "itch", "warm".
4. Keep going, one label at a time, at a lazy pace. There is nothing to achieve.

This practice is especially useful if you tend to wake in the night and find your mind already running. It is a close relative of the techniques in our guide to [calming your mind before bed](@quiet-the-mind/how-to-calm-your-mind-before-bed).

## Guided or unguided?

**Guided meditation** means following a recorded voice that tells you where to place your attention. For beginners it is easier, because the voice does the remembering and the pacing, and it gently interrupts a wandering mind every twenty seconds or so. The downside is that some voices grate, some recordings are too long or too chatty, and you become reliant on having a phone within reach at bedtime.

**Unguided (silent) meditation** takes more practice but has no dependencies. Once you know a practice well, you can use it at 3am without touching a device.

A sensible route is to start guided, learn the shape of one practice, and then try it silently on alternate nights. If you do listen in bed, comfort matters: ordinary earbuds press into the ear when you lie on your side, and a phone speaker pulls your partner into your routine. ## Apps and free options

There are plenty of meditation apps, most offering a free tier with a few sleep-focused sessions and a subscription for the rest. They are convenient and some are well made, but no app is necessary, and we do not endorse any particular one.

Free options worth knowing about: the NHS mindfulness page links to free resources and describes the approach used in NHS-recommended programmes; many public libraries offer audio through their app; and there are large collections of free guided meditations from university mindfulness centres. Our guide to [guided relaxation for sleep](@relaxation/guided-relaxation-for-sleep) explains how to choose a voice and a length.

Whatever you use, pick one or two recordings and stick with them. Browsing for the perfect session at 11pm is a form of screen time, and not a helpful one.

## What the evidence says

Mindfulness-based programmes have been tested for sleep in a fair number of trials. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Rusch and colleagues found that mindfulness meditation produced modest improvements in sleep quality compared with non-specific controls, with the effect holding up at follow-up. A well-known 2015 trial by Black and colleagues, in older adults with sleep problems, found that a six-week mindfulness course improved sleep quality more than a sleep hygiene education course.

The honest reading is that meditation helps some people, moderately, and that most of the evidence comes from structured programmes of several weeks rather than a single bedtime session. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) remains the first-line treatment for persistent insomnia, and meditation sits alongside it rather than replacing it.

## If meditation makes you more alert

This happens, and it is not a sign you are doing it wrong. Three common reasons:

- **You are meditating to make sleep happen**, and monitoring for it. Try practice three above, which is deliberately aimless, or shift the goal to "rest" rather than "sleep".
- **Focused attention wakes you up.** Some people find narrow concentration on the breath energising. Widen the focus: whole-body awareness, or sounds, rather than the nostrils.
- **You are not sleepy yet.** Meditation cannot create sleep pressure. If you are calm but wide awake, you may simply have gone to bed too early; a consistent [bedtime routine](@falling-asleep/bedtime-routine-for-adults) with a fixed wake time usually matters more than any technique.

If none of that helps, meditate earlier in the evening, sitting in a chair, and use something different in bed. Meditation practised in the daytime still tends to make nights calmer.
