White noise for sleep does one thing well: it hides the sounds that would otherwise wake you. If you live near a main road, share a wall with a noisy neighbour, or sleep beside someone who snores, a steady hiss in the background makes those sounds less likely to register. That is the whole mechanism, and it is a sound one.

What white noise does not do, at least not on current evidence, is improve sleep in a quiet room, deepen sleep or treat insomnia. The research on adults is small and mixed, with the clearest benefit showing up exactly where you would expect: in noisy environments.

Used at a sensible volume, it is considered safe for adults to use every night. The rest of this guide covers what white noise actually is, how to choose between a machine, an app and a fan, and the volume rule that matters more than any of that.

## What white noise is

White noise is random sound with equal energy at every frequency across the range of human hearing, in the same way that white light contains every visible colour. The result is a bright, even hiss, like an untuned analogue radio, a hairdryer or heavy rain on a conservatory roof.

Because our ears are most sensitive to higher frequencies, true white noise can sound sharp. That is why the other "colours" exist. Pink noise tilts the balance toward the low end and sounds like steady rain. [Brown noise](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-for-sleep) tilts further and sounds like a distant waterfall. All three mask noise; they differ mainly in how pleasant they are to have on for eight hours. If you are choosing between the two gentler options, [pink noise vs white noise](@sleep-sounds/pink-noise-vs-white-noise) sets them side by side. The [Sleep Sounds guides](@sleep-sounds) cover each in turn.

## How masking works

Your hearing does not switch off when you fall asleep. The brain keeps monitoring for anything that might matter, and what it reacts to most strongly is contrast: a sudden sound against a quiet background. A door closing in a silent flat is a large jump. The same door against a steady background is a much smaller one, and often does not cross the threshold that pulls you toward waking.

White noise raises the background. It does not remove the door, but it shrinks the jump. That is why it works best for intermittent noise (traffic, plumbing, a partner turning over, a fox in the garden) and less well for a constant sound that is already steady on its own.

If the noise is loud enough that you would need white noise at a high volume to cover it, sound alone is the wrong tool. Earplugs, heavier curtains and moving the bed away from the shared wall all reduce the noise reaching you rather than hiding it, and the [guide to blocking out noise at night](@sleep-environment/how-to-block-out-noise-at-night) walks through them. Many people combine a plug with a low level of sound, and the [earplugs vs white noise comparison](@sleep-environment/earplugs-vs-white-noise) helps you decide which to lead with.

> **In short:** White noise does not make a room quiet. It makes sudden sounds less sudden, which is what actually wakes you.

## What the research says

There is less good evidence than the popularity of white noise suggests.

A 2021 systematic review by Riedy and colleagues in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* gathered the studies on continuous noise as a sleep aid and rated the overall quality of evidence as low. Some studies found people fell asleep a little faster or woke less often; others found no effect; a few suggested noise slightly fragmented sleep. Studies differed in the sound used, the volume and who was tested, which makes them hard to compare.

The most encouraging results come from noisy settings. A small 2021 study by Ebben and colleagues in *Sleep Medicine* tested white noise with people living in a loud part of New York City and found they fell asleep faster with it on. Earlier work in hospital intensive care units, where noise is constant and unpredictable, pointed the same way.

So a fair reading is this. If noise is part of your problem, white noise is a reasonable, low-risk thing to try, and many people find it helps. If your bedroom is already quiet and you are awake because of a busy mind, it is unlikely to change much, and settling the mind is the better place to start.

## Machine, app or fan?

All three produce a usable masking sound. The differences are practical.

### A fan

A bedroom fan is white noise you may already own. It is steady, non-looping and cheap to run, and in summer the moving air is a bonus. In January, less so. There is also no volume control beyond the speed settings, and the sound changes if the fan oscillates.

### A phone app or long video

The cheapest way to find out whether you like the sound at all. The drawbacks are the phone's: notifications, adverts in free apps, a lit screen when you least want one, and a battery that has to last until morning. Choose a long track or a proper loop, switch on do-not-disturb, and keep the phone off the pillow.

### A dedicated machine

A machine has a physical dial, sits where you put it and never buzzes with a message. Fan-based machines use a real enclosed fan and produce a natural, continuous sound with no loop point. Digital machines generate white, pink and brown-style noise electronically and usually add a timer. Small rechargeable models are made for travel, where unfamiliar rooms are often the noisiest. ## The volume rule

This is the part that matters most, and the part most people get wrong.

White noise should be quieter than a normal conversation. Set it, then check that you could talk over it without raising your voice. If you are masking a specific sound, aim to soften it rather than drown it out; chasing silence leads to an ever-louder machine. You should still be able to hear a smoke alarm, a child, or someone knocking.

Prolonged exposure to loud sound damages hearing, and a sound that runs all night, every night, adds up in a way a loud hour does not. Ears that feel muffled or ring in the morning are a clear sign it is too loud. Be particularly careful with earbuds, which put the sound directly against the eardrum.

For babies, the advice is more cautious still. Sound machines placed in or near cots have been measured at levels that could harm developing hearing, so keep any device well away from the cot, at low volume, and follow NHS guidance on safe infant sleep.

## Will I become dependent on it?

Some people worry that once they start, they will not be able to sleep without it. What usually happens is gentler than that: the sound becomes one of the cues that tells your body it is time to settle, in the same way a familiar pillow does. That is a habit, and a harmless one, provided you can take a phone or small machine with you when you sleep away from home.

If the idea still bothers you, use a timer. Most people are asleep within half an hour, so a sound that fades after 60 minutes covers the useful part of the night while leaving you the reassurance that you can drop off in silence. If you have a fan or a phone, try it tonight at a volume you barely notice. Give it a week rather than a night, because the first night is as much about the novelty as the sound. If the hiss feels harsh, the [brown noise vs white noise comparison](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-vs-white-noise) explains the softer alternatives.
