Brown noise and white noise do the same job in different voices. Both are random, broadband sounds that cover up the sudden noises that would otherwise wake you. White noise spreads its energy evenly across every frequency and sounds like a bright hiss. Brown noise concentrates its energy at the low end and sounds like a deep, soft rumble.

For sleep, the honest answer is that neither has been shown to work better in a good trial. The research on noise as a sleep aid is small and mixed for both. What differs is how they feel to live with. White noise masks high-pitched sounds a little more effectively; brown noise is much easier to tolerate at low volume for eight hours, which is why most people who try both end up with brown.

This guide sets the two side by side, explains who each suits, and covers which devices play which.

## Brown noise vs white noise at a glance

| | White noise | Brown noise |
|---|---|---|
| **Sound character** | Bright, even hiss; untuned radio, hairdryer, heavy rain on a roof | Deep, soft rumble; distant waterfall, aircraft cabin, wind |
| **Frequency emphasis** | Equal energy at every frequency; sounds high because our ears are most sensitive there | Energy falls by about 6 dB per octave; almost no high frequencies |
| **How it masks** | Covers the widest range, including high-pitched sounds such as voices, clinking and birdsong | Covers low and mid sounds well: traffic, boilers, snoring, footsteps upstairs |
| **Best for** | Very noisy rooms; people who find a low rumble oppressive; hiding higher-pitched sounds | All-night use; people who find white noise harsh; light sleepers who want something they can ignore |
| **Potential downsides** | Can feel sharp or fatiguing; tempts people to turn it down to a level where it stops masking | Easy to turn up without noticing; may not cover the highest-pitched sounds; can feel heavy in a small room |
| **Where the name comes from** | By analogy with white light, which contains every colour | Brownian motion, after the botanist Robert Brown; nothing to do with the colour |

The name matters more than it seems, because it explains the sound. Brown noise is generated by a random walk, the same mathematics that describes the jittering of pollen grains in water that Brown observed in 1827. A random walk changes slowly, which is why the sound is dominated by slow, low-frequency movement. Pink noise sits between the two, falling by about 3 decibels per octave, and is compared separately in [pink noise vs white noise](@sleep-sounds/pink-noise-vs-white-noise).

## How they feel through a night

**White noise** is what most people picture when they think of a sleep machine, and it is what fan-based machines produce. Its strength is coverage: because it has energy at every frequency, there is no sound it cannot help to hide. Its weakness is that the same high-frequency energy is what our ears find sharp. Some people love it; a lot of people find it fine for twenty minutes and wearing by 2am. Fan-based machines soften the effect, because a real fan produces something closer to pink than pure white.

**Brown noise** has the high frequencies rolled off almost entirely. What is left is the kind of sound we tend not to notice: surf, wind, an engine in the next carriage. Because it is not sharp, most people can run it quietly and still feel covered, and it rarely becomes wearing. The trade-off is that a very high-pitched sound, a squeaky hinge or a distant alarm, is less well hidden. The [brown noise guide](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-for-sleep) goes into the detail.

> **In short:** White noise covers more; brown noise is easier to live with. For all-night use in an ordinary bedroom, easier to live with usually wins.

## Which suits which problem

### Traffic, neighbours and boilers

Most of the noise that gets into a UK bedroom is low and mid frequency: road noise, footsteps through a ceiling, a boiler firing, a washing machine on a late cycle. Brown noise sits in the same range and blends these in well. If the noise is loud enough that you would have to turn either sound up to cover it, sound alone is the wrong tool, and the [guide to blocking out noise at night](@sleep-environment/how-to-block-out-noise-at-night) covers the fixes that reduce it instead.

### A snoring partner

Snoring is mostly low-to-mid frequency with a rhythm, and brown noise usually blends it in better. Some people find the rasping, higher part of a snore still cuts through and prefer white. Either way, the [guide to sleeping with a snoring partner](@sleep-environment/how-to-sleep-with-a-snoring-partner) covers what to do beyond sound.

### Light sleepers

If you wake at every creak, the goal is a sound you can completely ignore, and brown noise is generally that sound. White noise at a very low level can also work, but at the level most light sleepers want it, it tends to become something they notice. There is more on the wider approach in the [light sleeper guide](@waking-at-night/light-sleeper).

### A busy mind rather than a noisy room

If the room is quiet and it is your thoughts keeping you awake, neither sound is a fix. Brown noise gives attention something soft to rest on, and some people find that helps them drift, but it will not settle a racing mind on its own. That needs a different approach, and one that deals with the thoughts rather than the room.

### High-pitched sounds

Birdsong at dawn, a squeaking gate, a neighbour's television dialogue: these live in the range brown noise barely touches. White or pink noise covers them better.

## How to try both

Do not decide on one night. Play each for three or four nights at a low volume, well below the level of normal conversation, and notice how you feel on waking rather than how it sounded when you pressed play. A phone app is the cheapest way to run the comparison. If you find yourself turning brown noise up and up, that is a sign to step back to pink or white, which do more masking per decibel. If white noise makes you tense, go the other way.

Whichever you choose, keep it quiet. Prolonged loud sound damages hearing, and all night is prolonged; if your ears feel dull in the morning, it is too loud. The rest of the [Sleep Sounds guides](@sleep-sounds) cover the natural alternatives, which are mostly variations on the same spectrum with a little texture added.

## Which devices play each

**Fan-based machines** make white noise, or something close to it, with a real fan. The sound never loops, and there is no screen or app, but it is one colour only and cannot produce brown noise.

**Digital sound machines** generate white, pink and brown variants electronically, usually with a timer and a choice of fan sounds. They are the obvious choice if you want to compare the two and settle on one. The [white noise machine buying guide](@sleep-products/best-white-noise-machines-uk) explains the trade-offs.

**Sleep headphones** play whichever sound you like from a phone, and keep it to yourself in a shared bed. Headband styles suit side sleepers because nothing sits inside the ear; small sleep earbuds add some passive blocking as well. The [sleep headphones guide](@sleep-products/best-sleep-headphones-uk) compares them.

## The short version

If you have never used sleep sound before, start with brown noise at a volume you barely notice; it is the one most people find easiest to keep on. If you need to hide higher-pitched sounds, or brown feels heavy, move to pink and then white. Give each a few nights, keep it quiet, and remember that the goal is a sound you forget is there.
