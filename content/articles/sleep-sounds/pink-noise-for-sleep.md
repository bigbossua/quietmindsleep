Pink noise is a steady, broadband sound with more energy at the low end than the high. To most ears it sounds like rain falling evenly on a roof, a waterfall heard from a path, or wind moving through trees. It is the sound many "white noise" apps and machines actually produce, because pure white noise is harsher than most people want in a bedroom.

For sleep, pink noise works the way every steady sound works: it masks the sudden noises that would otherwise wake you. There is also a strand of research suggesting that carefully timed pulses of pink noise can boost the slow brain waves of deep sleep. That work is interesting but small, done in laboratories with special equipment, and not the same as leaving a track on overnight. This guide explains both, and how to use pink noise sensibly.

## What pink noise is

The colours of noise describe how random sound is spread across frequencies.

White noise has equal energy at every frequency. Because our hearing is most sensitive to higher pitches, it comes across as a bright hiss.

Pink noise has equal energy in each octave instead. Since each octave up covers twice the range of frequencies, the energy per frequency falls as you go higher, by about 3 decibels per octave. The result is balanced to the ear: the high hiss is softened and the low end is present without being boomy.

[Brown noise](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-for-sleep) goes one step further, falling by about 6 decibels per octave, and sounds like a low rumble with almost no hiss at all. Pink sits between the two, which is why it is often the first alternative to try when white noise feels too sharp.

## Why it sounds like rain

Rain, surf, wind and rustling leaves all have roughly this shape: a broad spread of frequencies with the balance tipped toward the low end and no single tone standing out. That is why pink noise sounds "natural" while white noise sounds electronic. It is also why many people who cannot stand a white noise machine happily fall asleep to a [rain recording](@sleep-sounds/rain-sounds-for-sleep), which is close to pink noise with a little texture added.

The lack of any single tone matters. A sound with a distinct pitch, a hum from a fridge or a whistle from a fan, gives your attention something to hold on to. Pink noise offers nothing to latch on to, so attention slides off it, which is exactly what you want when you are trying to let go of the day.

> **In short:** Pink noise is the natural-sounding middle option: less harsh than white, less heavy than brown, and the closest of the three to steady rain.

## What the evidence says

It helps to separate two different questions.

### Does pink noise mask disruptive sound?

Yes, in the same way white and brown noise do. A steady background raises the floor, so a slamming door or a passing motorbike is a smaller jump and less likely to wake you. This is straightforward acoustics rather than a claim about pink noise in particular, and it is the reason steady sound suits light sleepers and anyone in a noisy flat. If noise is your main problem, pair sound with the practical fixes in [how to block out noise at night](@sleep-environment/how-to-block-out-noise-at-night).

### Does pink noise deepen sleep?

This is the claim that gets pink noise into headlines, and it deserves a careful answer.

In 2013, Ngo and colleagues published a study in *Neuron* in which short bursts of pink noise were played to sleeping volunteers, timed precisely to the rise of their slow brain waves using an EEG. The pulses made the slow waves larger and improved memory of word pairs learned the evening before. In 2017, Papalambros and colleagues reported a similar effect in a small group of older adults in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.

These are genuine, interesting results. But notice what they involve: brief pulses, delivered at exact moments picked out by brain monitoring, in a sleep lab, to a handful of people. None of that is what happens when you leave a pink noise track running from your phone. Continuous pink noise has not been shown to deepen sleep in the same way, and a 2021 systematic review by Riedy and colleagues in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* rated the evidence for continuous noise as a sleep aid as low quality and mixed.

So the honest summary is that pink noise is a pleasant, effective masking sound, and the deep-sleep story is promising but unproven for everyday use. If you sleep better with it on, that is reason enough. Just do not expect it to do something a good bedtime routine would not.

## How to use pink noise at night

**Start with a phone.** Pink noise tracks are easy to find and free. Use a long track rather than a short loop, switch on do-not-disturb and put the phone face down or across the room.

**Move to a machine if it sticks.** A dedicated digital sound machine generates pink noise continuously, has a real volume dial and usually a timer. Fan-based machines make a natural white-to-pink sound with an actual fan, so check which you are getting. The [white noise machine buying guide](@sleep-products/best-white-noise-machines-uk) explains the difference.

**Keep it quiet.** Pink noise should be well below the level of normal conversation. You are trying to soften outside sounds, not bury them. Prolonged loud sound damages hearing over time, and a track that runs all night counts as prolonged. If your ears feel dull in the morning, it is too loud. Take particular care with earbuds.

**Use a timer if you prefer.** If you would rather not have sound all night, a fade-out after 45 to 60 minutes covers the time it usually takes to fall asleep. If you tend to wake in the small hours and struggle to get back off, running it all night at a low level may suit you better, since the masking is doing its work at 3am as much as at 11pm.

**Give it a week.** The first night with any new sound is partly about novelty. Judge it after five or six nights, and pay attention to how you feel on waking rather than how quickly you think you dropped off.

**Babies are different.** Keep any sound device well away from a cot and at a low volume, and follow NHS guidance on safe infant sleep.
