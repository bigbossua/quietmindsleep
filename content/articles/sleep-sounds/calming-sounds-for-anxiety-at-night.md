When anxiety arrives at night, the problem is rarely silence. It is that in silence, attention has nowhere to go except inward, and inward is where the worry lives. Calming sounds help by giving attention something steady and undemanding to rest on instead. Steady noise, slow music and a calm recorded voice all work in this way, and which one suits you depends on how loud your thoughts are.

It is worth saying plainly that sound is not a treatment for anxiety. It will not resolve what is worrying you, and if anxiety is keeping you awake most nights, that deserves proper support. What sound can do is make the next twenty minutes easier, and often that is enough to let sleep arrive. This guide covers the three kinds of sound that help, how to pair them with breathing, and when to look for more than a soundtrack.

## Why sound helps an anxious mind

An anxious mind at night is running a search: what did I miss, what happens tomorrow, why is my heart going like that. Attention is pulled inward and the body responds as if the threat were real. Telling yourself to stop does not work, because the instruction is just another thought.

What does work, gently, is giving attention an anchor: something outside the head that is steady, harmless and slightly interesting. Sound is a good anchor because you do not have to do anything to receive it. You can lie in the dark with your eyes closed and let it be there. Each time attention drifts back to the worry, the sound is still going, and you can return to it without effort or judgement. This is the same principle behind [mindfulness for sleep](@relaxation/mindfulness-for-sleep), with the sound doing the work that the breath usually does.

> **In short:** Sound does not silence anxious thoughts. It gives your attention somewhere else to rest, so the thoughts have less of your company.

## Three kinds of sound that help

### Steady noise

Brown noise, pink noise, rain or gentle surf: continuous, low-tilted sounds with nothing in them to notice. These suit people whose thoughts are loud and fast, because the sound asks nothing of you and there is no melody or voice to follow. They also mask the small noises, a creak, a car door, that an anxious brain reads as significant. [Brown noise](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-for-sleep) is the usual starting point; it is the softest of the noise colours and the easiest to leave on.

### Slow music

Music with a slow pulse, no lyrics and little variation, whether ambient, quiet piano or long sustained strings, can be more comforting than plain noise because it carries a little warmth. The risk is that music you know invites you to listen and anticipate, which is a form of engagement rather than rest. Choose something unfamiliar, slow and long, and keep it low enough that it sits at the edge of hearing.

### A calm voice

Guided relaxation, a body scan or a sleep story gives a mind that will not stop talking something else to follow. For some people this is the most effective option of the three, because a voice occupies the same channel the worry uses. The [guided relaxation guide](@relaxation/guided-relaxation-for-sleep) explains what to look for. Keep the volume low, choose a recording that ends quietly rather than with an advert, and do not worry if you fall asleep halfway through; that is the point.

## Pairing sound with breathing

Sound on its own settles the mind. Adding slow breathing settles the body, which is where anxiety at night tends to be felt most: the racing heart, the tight chest, the restlessness in the legs.

The simplest pairing is to let the out-breath lengthen while the sound plays, without counting anything. Breathe in normally, then let the breath leave slowly, as if you were fogging a mirror. Ocean recordings make this easy because the waves set a slow rhythm you can follow. If you like structure, the [breathing exercises for sleep](@relaxation/breathing-exercises-for-sleep) offer several patterns; pick one and stay with it rather than switching between them.

Do not try to relax. Trying is effort, and effort is the opposite of what you are after. Let the sound be there, let the out-breath be slow, and let whatever happens happen.

## Playing sound at night

A phone works, but notifications, bright screens and adverts are exactly what an anxious mind does not need at 2am. If you use one, switch on do-not-disturb, choose a long track, and put it face down out of reach.

If you share a bed, or want the sound close and private, sleep headphones make the difference between a soundtrack you can use and one you cannot. Headband styles keep flat speakers against the ears with nothing inside the ear, which suits side sleepers; small sleep earbuds add a little passive blocking as well. The [sleep headphones guide](@sleep-products/best-sleep-headphones-uk) compares the types.

Whatever you use, keep the volume low. Prolonged loud sound damages hearing, and the aim is a sound you barely notice rather than one that drowns out thought.

## When sound is not enough

Sound is a support for a difficult night. It is not a plan for anxiety. If you are lying awake with worry more nights than not, if the anxiety is there in the day as well, or if you wake in the night with a pounding heart, it is worth reading about [anxiety at night](@quiet-the-mind/anxiety-at-night) and considering a conversation with a GP. Talking therapies for anxiety and for insomnia are available on the NHS, and Mind has clear information on both.

Persistent sleep problems have their own guidance in [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep). Asking is not an over-reaction; it is the sensible next step when the nightly toolkit stops being enough.

## Tonight's version

Choose one sound: brown noise if your thoughts are loud, a calm voice if they will not stop talking. Set it low. Let the out-breath lengthen for a few minutes without counting. When attention goes back to the worry, and it will, return to the sound without comment. The rest of the [Sleep Sounds guides](@sleep-sounds) can help you find the right sound once the night is over.
