Pink noise and white noise are close relatives. Both are random sound spread across every frequency we can hear, and both mask the sudden noises that would otherwise wake you. The difference is balance. White noise has equal energy at every frequency and sounds like a bright hiss. Pink noise has equal energy in every octave, which tilts it toward the low end and makes it sound like steady rain.

For sleep, that tilt is most of the story. Neither has been shown to be better in a good trial, and the research on both is small and mixed. But pink noise is noticeably easier to have on all night, and many machines and apps labelled "white noise" are in fact producing something closer to pink for that reason. If white noise has ever felt harsh to you, pink is the first thing to try.

## Pink noise vs white noise at a glance

| | White noise | Pink noise |
|---|---|---|
| **Sound character** | Bright, even hiss; untuned radio, hairdryer | Softer, fuller; steady rain, a waterfall from a distance, wind in trees |
| **Frequency emphasis** | Equal energy at every frequency, so it sounds high-pitched to human ears | Equal energy per octave; falls by about 3 dB per octave, so highs are softened and lows are present |
| **How it masks** | Covers the widest range, including high-pitched sounds | Covers most everyday noise; slightly less strong at the very top of the range |
| **Best for** | Very noisy rooms; hiding voices, clinks and other high sounds | All-night use; people who find white noise sharp; anyone who likes rain sounds but wants no texture |
| **Potential downsides** | Can feel sharp or fatiguing over hours | Slightly less coverage of high-pitched sounds; can still feel busy to people who prefer a deep rumble |
| **Evidence** | Masking is well established; sleep-quality studies small and mixed | Masking is well established; lab studies of timed pulses are promising but not the same as playing a track |

If pink noise still feels too bright, brown noise goes a step further, falling by about 6 decibels per octave, and is compared in [brown noise vs white noise](@sleep-sounds/brown-noise-vs-white-noise).

## Why the balance matters

Human hearing is most sensitive in the range where speech consonants sit, roughly 2,000 to 5,000 hertz. White noise has as much energy there as anywhere else, so that region dominates what you hear, and it is the region we tend to find piercing. Pink noise cuts that energy back with every octave, and the result is a sound that most people describe as natural rather than electronic.

This has a practical effect. A harsh sound keeps a thread of your attention on it, and attention is what you are trying to let go of at bedtime. A sound you can forget lets you drift. Pink noise is also easier to run quietly, because you are not fighting the urge to turn a hiss down to the point where it stops masking anything.

> **In short:** White noise covers slightly more; pink noise is much easier to forget about. For a bedroom, the second usually matters more.

## What the evidence says

Both sounds mask. That is not in doubt: a steady background sound makes a sudden noise a smaller jump and less likely to wake you. It is why either helps in a flat by a main road or beside a snoring partner, and why the [guide to blocking out noise at night](@sleep-environment/how-to-block-out-noise-at-night) suggests pairing sound with things that physically reduce noise.

Beyond masking, the evidence is thin for both. A 2021 systematic review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* by Riedy and colleagues rated the studies of continuous noise as a sleep aid as low quality, with results pointing both ways.

Pink noise has one extra claim attached to it. Laboratory studies, including a 2017 study by Papalambros and colleagues in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, played brief pulses of pink noise timed to the slow brain waves of deep sleep and found the waves grew larger, with a small improvement in overnight memory. This is interesting, but it involved precisely timed pulses delivered by equipment reading the sleeper's brain activity. Continuous pink noise from a phone or machine has not been shown to do the same. The [pink noise guide](@sleep-sounds/pink-noise-for-sleep) covers this in more depth.

So choose between them on comfort, not on the hope of deeper sleep.

## Which suits you

**Choose white noise if** your room is genuinely loud, the noise includes high-pitched sounds such as voices or a television through the wall, or you have a fan-based machine and find it fine. Fan-based machines produce a real fan sound that sits between white and pink anyway.

**Choose pink noise if** white noise has ever felt sharp, you like rain sounds but find recordings distracting, or you want something you can run all night at a low level and forget. The [rain sounds guide](@sleep-sounds/rain-sounds-for-sleep) is the natural next step if you want a little texture back.

**Choose brown noise if** even pink feels busy and you want a deep, even rumble with nothing in it to notice.

Whichever you pick, keep the volume well below normal conversation. Prolonged loud sound damages hearing, and eight hours a night is prolonged. If your ears feel dull in the morning, it is too loud. For babies, keep any device well away from the cot at a low volume and follow NHS safe-sleep guidance.

## Which devices play each

Fan-based machines make one sound, a natural fan hiss, and cannot switch colour. Digital sound machines generate white, pink and brown variants, usually with a timer, and are the sensible choice if you want to compare and settle. Phone apps play all three but bring notifications, adverts and a lit screen to the bedside. ## The short version

Try pink noise first, at a volume you barely notice, for three or four nights. If it covers what you need and you stop noticing it, you are done. If high-pitched sounds still get through, step up to white. If it feels busy, step down to brown. The right sound is the one you forget is on.
