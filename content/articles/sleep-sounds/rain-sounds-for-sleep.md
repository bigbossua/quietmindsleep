Rain sounds help people sleep for three overlapping reasons. The sound itself is steady and broad, so it masks the sudden noises that wake you. It has the soft, low-tilted balance of pink noise, which most ears find easy to ignore. And it comes with associations: being indoors, the day called off, nothing you can do about it now.

That last part is why rain does something for many people that a machine hiss does not, even though the two sounds are acoustically close. There is no strong evidence that rain sounds improve sleep beyond that masking and settling effect, but there is also little downside if you use them at a low volume. This guide covers how to choose a recording, the thunder question, and how to play rain at night without a bright screen and an advert at 3am.

## Why rain is easy to sleep to

### It is steady

What wakes you is contrast: a sudden sound against a quiet background. Rain fills in the background, so a car door or a creaking pipe is a smaller jump. This is the same masking effect that makes [white noise](@sleep-sounds/white-noise-for-sleep) useful, and it works best on intermittent sounds rather than a constant drone.

### It has a gentle spectrum

Rain on a roof or pavement spreads its energy across many frequencies, with more at the low end and no single tone standing out. That is roughly the profile of [pink noise](@sleep-sounds/pink-noise-for-sleep), and it is why rain rarely feels harsh in the way a pure white hiss can. Because there is no pitch to latch on to, your attention slides off it. [Ocean sounds](@sleep-sounds/ocean-sounds-for-sleep) share the same soft profile but add a slow rise and fall that some people prefer to rain's evenness.

### It is not a threat

Our hearing keeps a watch through the night, and sounds that might mean something, footsteps, voices, a bark, are the ones that pull us toward waking. Rain means nothing needs doing. For most people who grew up in a wet climate, it also carries a lifetime of associations with being warm and inside. That learned sense of safety is probably as important as the acoustics.

> **In short:** Rain works as a sleep sound because it masks, because it is soft in the frequencies our ears find sharp, and because it signals that there is nothing to attend to.

## Choosing a recording

Not all rain tracks are equal, and the differences matter more at 3am than they do when you first press play.

**Long recordings beat short loops.** A one-minute loop repeated for eight hours has a join, and once you have noticed it you will listen for it. Look for genuinely long recordings, or for generated rain that never repeats. If a track has a visible waveform, an obvious pattern is a warning sign.

**Even is better than eventful.** Rain that swells and fades, with gusts and drips and gutters overflowing, is lovely to listen to and less good to sleep to, because each change draws a little attention. Steady, medium rain with little variation is the sleep-friendly choice.

**Mind the drips.** Some recordings feature a single loud drip or a tap on a window at regular intervals. It sounds atmospheric; it is also exactly the kind of repeated sharp sound that masking is meant to hide.

**Rain on what?** Rain on a tent or a car roof is louder and more percussive. Rain on leaves or a distant street is softer and closer to pink noise. Try both; people differ.

**Does it need to be real?** Not really. Generated rain, built from filtered noise, has no join and no surprises, and many people cannot tell the difference at low volume. Recorded rain has more texture. If you find yourself listening to a recording rather than ignoring it, the generated version may serve you better.

## Thunder or not?

Thunderstorm tracks are popular, and a distant, low rumble can be part of a pleasant soundscape. Sharp, close thunder is a different matter. A crack of thunder is a sudden loud sound, which is precisely what you are playing rain to cover up. If you find storms comforting, choose a recording where the thunder is far off and rolling, and keep the volume low enough that the loudest moment is still quiet. If you tend to wake easily, leave the thunder out altogether. The [nature sounds guide](@sleep-sounds/nature-sounds-for-sleep) goes through which other soundscapes hide the same trap.

## Where to play it

**A phone** is the easiest place to start, and where most people begin. The problems are the usual ones: a screen lighting up, notifications, an advert breaking in when a free video ends, and a battery that has to last the night. If you use a phone, pick a long track, switch on do-not-disturb, and put it face down out of reach. Some paid apps and streaming services offer ad-free rain that runs indefinitely.

**A sound machine** avoids all of that. Digital machines often include a rain setting alongside white, pink and brown noise, and the better ones generate it continuously rather than looping. Small rechargeable models suit travel and hotel rooms. The [white noise machine buying guide](@sleep-products/best-white-noise-machines-uk) explains what to look for.

**Sleep headphones** are the answer if you share a bed with someone who wants silence, or want the rain close without filling the room. Headband styles keep flat speakers against your ears without pushing anything into the ear when you lie on your side, and small sleep earbuds add a little passive blocking of their own. The [sleep headphones guide](@sleep-products/best-sleep-headphones-uk) compares the types.

## Keep it quiet

Rain sounds are easy to turn up, because they never feel loud in the way a hiss does. Keep the volume below normal conversation, quiet enough that you could still hear a smoke alarm or a child. Prolonged loud sound damages hearing, and eight hours a night is prolonged. Take particular care with earbuds. For a baby, keep any device well away from the cot at a low volume and follow NHS safe-sleep guidance.
