The best cooling pillow in the UK is the one whose cooling method suits how you actually sleep hot. If your head feels warm and clammy against the pillow, breathability matters most. If you like the sink of memory foam but it traps heat, a gel-layered or ventilated foam is the compromise. If you want the cool-to-the-touch feeling when you first lie down, a phase-change cover delivers that, briefly.

The honest thing to say up front is that no pillow keeps your head cool all night. The cooling effect of most designs is strongest at first contact and fades as the material reaches body temperature. That is still worth having, because a cooler start helps you fall asleep, and a pillow that does not trap heat helps you stay asleep. But if you are [waking up hot at night](@waking-at-night/waking-up-hot-at-night), the pillow is one of several things to change and rarely the biggest.

## Why the pillow matters when you sleep hot

Your body cools itself as you fall asleep; a drop in core temperature is part of the process, and heat escapes largely through the skin of the head, hands and feet. The pillow sits under one of those areas for the whole night, and a dense foam pillow acts like a hat: it stops heat leaving. That is why hot sleepers keep flipping the pillow to the cool side. A pillow that lets heat and moisture escape removes the need.

The limit is obvious once you say it. A pillow can only let heat out; it cannot lower the temperature of a warm room. On a July night in a top-floor flat, a cooling pillow on its own will disappoint. The bigger levers are the room, the duvet and airflow, covered in [how to sleep when it's hot](@sleep-environment/how-to-sleep-when-its-hot). For the target to aim at, see [the best temperature for sleep](@sleep-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep).

> **In short:** A cooling pillow stops your head overheating against the pillow. It does not cool the room, and the room is usually the problem.

## How cooling claims work

### Gel layers and gel-infused foam

The most common design. A layer of gel on top of memory foam, or gel beads mixed into the foam, absorbs heat from your head faster than plain foam does, so it feels cool at first. Once the gel reaches skin temperature, the effect fades; how quickly depends on the thickness and the room. Gel-infused foam is generally less dramatic than a gel top layer but more even through the night. These pillows keep the contouring support of memory foam, which is why they suit people who like foam but not the heat.

### Breathable and ventilated fills

Shredded foam, foam with drilled air channels, latex with a honeycomb structure, buckwheat hulls, and blends of wool or down let air move through the pillow so heat and moisture escape. They rarely feel cold to the touch, but they stay noticeably less warm at 3am than a solid block. For many hot sleepers, this quieter kind of cooling is the more useful one.

### Phase-change covers

Fabrics treated with materials that absorb heat as they melt at around skin temperature, marketed as cool-to-the-touch. They deliver the most obvious cool sensation when you first lie down and can help you drop off, but the effect is short and it is hard to know from a listing how much of the material is present or how long it lasts through washing.

### Marketing you can discount

Terms such as 'ice silk', 'arctic' and 'cooling technology' are not standardised, and cooling claims are hard to compare between brands because there is no common test. Look for a plain description of the mechanism, gel, ventilation or a treated cover, rather than a temperature promise. Any pillow that claims to lower your body temperature or to cool the room is overreaching.

## Pillowcases and protectors

The fabric between your face and the pillow decides much of how a pillow feels. A polyester or sateen pillowcase holds warmth and moisture; a percale cotton, linen or bamboo-viscose case lets both escape and feels crisper. Many cooling pillows arrive with a plastic-backed or quilted polyester protector that undoes most of the pillow's breathability; swap it for a thin cotton protector or none at all. If you like the cold-side feeling, two pillowcases in rotation, one on the pillow and one folded in a cool cupboard, is the cheapest cooling technology there is.

## What to look for

- **Mechanism that matches your problem:** breathability for clamminess, gel over foam if you want foam support, a phase-change cover for the first-contact cool.
- **A removable, washable cover** in a breathable fabric such as cotton or bamboo-viscose. Hot sleepers sweat more, and a cover you can wash weekly is a hygiene point as much as a comfort one.
- **The right height and firmness for how you sleep.** Cooling is no use if the loft is wrong; side sleepers in particular should read the [side sleeper pillow guide](@sleep-products/best-pillows-for-side-sleepers-uk) first.
- **Weight.** Gel-layered foam pillows can be heavy and hold their shape; if you like to fold or scrunch a pillow, they will frustrate you.
- **A trial period.** Cooling is felt, not measured, and a couple of warm nights tell you more than any description.
- **Care instructions.** Most foam and gel cannot be washed; the cover can.

## Who it suits, and who should skip it

A cooling pillow makes sense for people who wake with a warm, damp head, who flip the pillow repeatedly, who like foam support but find it hot, or who sleep in a room that is hard to cool. It also suits people going through the menopause or on medication that makes them run warm, as one small, easy change alongside the bigger ones.

Skip it if your room is the problem and you have not yet dealt with that, if you sleep comfortably on a light fibre pillow already, or if you are hoping a pillow will fix night sweats. New, drenching or unexplained night sweats are worth mentioning to a GP; the NHS has a plain page on the common causes.

## Free ways to sleep cooler tonight

Swap the pillowcase for cotton or linen and put a spare in the fridge for a few minutes before bed if you like the first-contact cool. Take the pillow protector off if it is a plastic-backed one, which traps heat. Open the window on the shaded side of the flat and the bedroom door to get air moving. Change to a summer-weight duvet or just a sheet. Move the pillow to the cooler end of the bed, away from the radiator or the wall that faces the afternoon sun. The [bedding and sleep guide](@sleep-environment/bedding-and-sleep) covers togs and fabrics in more depth.
