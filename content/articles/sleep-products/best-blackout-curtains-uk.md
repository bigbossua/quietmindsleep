The best blackout curtains for a UK bedroom are not the thickest or the most expensive. They are the ones that cover the whole window with room to spare, so light cannot creep in around the edges, above the pole or through the gap in the middle. Almost every disappointing blackout curtain fails on coverage, not on fabric.

So the decision is mostly about the window. Curtains hang outside the recess and can overlap generously on all sides. Blinds fit inside the recess and stop the glow at the very edges. Liners turn the curtains you already own into blackout ones. For a genuinely dark room in a house with a street light outside, the answer is often a blind and curtains together.

This guide explains what darkness does for sleep, the difference between the options, how to measure, what to look for on the label, and who each suits. Examples follow the advice.

## Why a dark bedroom helps, and where curtains fall short

Your body clock takes its main cue from light. Light in the evening delays it; light in the early morning advances it and starts the process of waking. In the UK that means a summer dawn at 4.30am can pull you awake hours early, and a sodium or LED street light can keep a room in permanent dusk. If you tend to [wake too early](@waking-at-night/waking-up-too-early) in May and June, the window is the first place to look.

Blackout curtains fix the room rather than the person. That is their great advantage over a mask: they help everyone in the bed, they need no wearing or washing, and they double as insulation. Their limits are the gaps. Fabric that blocks nearly all light is easy to buy, yet light still finds its way in above the pole, down each edge and between the two panels. Curtains also do nothing for a glowing charger, a landing light or a partner's tablet, and they are a semi-permanent fitting, which matters if you rent. If you are weighing a mask against curtains, [sleep mask vs blackout curtains](@sleep-environment/sleep-mask-vs-blackout-curtains) puts the two side by side.

> **In short:** Blackout fabric is standard; coverage is what you are buying. Overlap the frame, deal with the top, and consider a blind inside the recess.

## Curtains, blinds and liners

### Blackout curtains

A dense woven fabric or a coated backing bonded to a decorative face fabric. They hang from a pole or track outside the window recess and can extend well beyond it in every direction, which is their strength. Thermal versions add extra layers marketed for insulation. Choose a pole that extends 15 to 20 cm beyond the frame on each side, and a drop that reaches the floor or sill, so the edges can be pushed back against the wall.

### Blackout blinds

Roller, cassette or honeycomb blinds with blackout fabric, fitted inside the recess. They sit close to the glass and stop the halo of light at the edges that curtains leave. Ordinary roller blinds still leak a little at the sides where the fabric clears the channel; cassette blinds with side channels are darker. No-drill versions attach with suction cups or tension fittings and suit rented homes, travel cots and holiday lets, though they may need re-fixing occasionally and look less tidy.

### Blackout liners

A plain blackout layer that hooks onto the back of your existing curtains or hangs from a second track. The cheapest route to a dark room if you like your curtains and your pole is strong enough for the extra weight. Coverage is limited to what the existing curtains already cover.

### Eyelet versus pencil pleat

Eyelet curtains thread straight onto a pole and hang in wide, even folds. They are easy to fit and to draw, but the top of the curtain sits below the pole, so a strip of light gets in over it. Pencil pleat curtains hang from hooks on a track or pole rings and can sit tighter to the wall and higher; with a track and a pelmet they close the top gap best. If you cannot fit a pelmet, hang eyelet curtains from a pole mounted well above the frame so the over-the-top light lands on the wall, not the bed.

## How to measure

Measure the width of the window including the frame, then add 30 to 40 cm so the curtains can overlap on both sides. Pole width should be that total, with the finials extra. For the drop, measure from the top of the pole (eyelet) or the hook position (pencil pleat) to where you want the curtain to end; to the floor blocks more light than to the sill. For a blind inside the recess, measure the recess width at three heights and use the narrowest; for a blind outside the recess, add at least 5 cm on each side.

Curtain widths are sold per panel, so a pair described as 168 cm wide gives you two panels of that width. For pencil pleat you want total panel width around twice the pole length for good fullness; eyelet needs less, roughly one and a half times.

## What to look for

- **Coverage first.** Enough width to overlap, enough drop to reach the floor or sill, a pole or track wider than the frame.
- **Fabric described as blackout, not 'dim-out' or 'room darkening'.** Those terms mean partial.
- **Thermal lining** if the window is draughty or single-glazed; it will earn its keep in winter and help keep sun off the room in summer, which matters when [the bedroom is hot](@sleep-environment/how-to-sleep-when-its-hot).
- **Washability.** Coated backings usually mean cool wash or dry clean; check the label.
- **Heading type** to suit your pole or track, and a pole strong enough for the weight of a lined pair.
- **Blind side channels** if you go the blind route and edge glow is your problem.
- **Safety with cords.** Any blind with a cord or chain in a home with children needs a cord tidy or a cordless design.

## Who they suit, and who should look elsewhere

Blackout curtains suit anyone whose bedroom faces a street light, a security light or the sunrise, shift workers who sleep in the day, and anyone sharing with a light-sensitive partner. They are also worth it for insulation alone in a cold or draughty room.

Look elsewhere if you rent and cannot fit a pole, in which case a no-drill blind or a [sleep mask](@sleep-products/best-sleep-masks-uk) does the job without holes in the wall. If the light in your room comes from inside, such as a landing light, a router or a charger, curtains will not fix it; our guide to [bedroom lighting for sleep](@sleep-environment/bedroom-lighting-for-sleep) covers bulbs, lamps and the small glowing things.

## Free ways to close the gaps

Before spending anything, pin the outer edges of your existing curtains to the wall with a clip or a strip of hook-and-loop tape so they lie flat. Overlap the two panels in the middle with a clothes peg or a curtain magnet. Fold a towel along the top of the pole to catch the light coming over it. Move the bed so the glow from the edges does not fall on your pillow.

Then go round the room: charger lights, the standby LED on the television, the router, the gap under the door. The full checklist is in [how to make your bedroom darker](@sleep-environment/how-to-make-bedroom-darker).
