A weighted blanket for sleep is a quilted blanket filled with glass beads or plastic pellets, sewn into small pockets so the weight sits evenly across your body. People who like them describe the feeling as being held. Whether that translates into better sleep is a fair question, and the honest answer is: for some people, probably, and the research is still catching up.

If you already find a heavy winter duvet comforting, or you settle more easily when something presses evenly on you, a weighted blanket is a reasonable thing to try. If you sleep hot, move a lot, have breathing or mobility difficulties, or are shopping for a young child, it is not the right product, and this guide will tell you why before it tells you what to buy.

Below: what the evidence says, how to choose the weight and fabric, who should avoid one, then a product example and the free alternatives.

## What the evidence actually says

The idea behind weighted blankets is deep pressure: steady, even pressure across the body that some people find calming, in the same way a firm hug can be. It is a technique occupational therapists have used for years, particularly with autistic people and people with sensory processing differences.

For sleep in the general population the research is small and mixed. The most cited study is a Swedish randomised trial (Ekholm, Spulber and Adler, Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2020) in adults with insomnia alongside conditions such as depression, anxiety or ADHD, which found larger improvements in reported insomnia and daytime symptoms with a weighted chain blanket than with a light control blanket. Other small studies have found calmer ratings or reduced anxiety in specific settings. Reviews of this evidence generally conclude that the results are encouraging but that the trials are few, small and short, and that better ones are needed.

So the fair summary is: a weighted blanket may help you feel calmer at bedtime and may improve how you rate your sleep, especially if anxiety is part of the picture. It is not a treatment for insomnia, anxiety or any other condition, and it does not replace the approaches with stronger evidence, such as CBT-I for persistent insomnia. If [anxiety at night](@quiet-the-mind/anxiety-at-night) is what you are dealing with, that guide explains the mechanism and the steps with the best track record; a blanket can sit alongside them.

> **In short:** Some people find a weighted blanket calming, and a few studies support a modest benefit for sleep and anxiety. It is a comfort aid, not a cure, and it suits some bodies better than others.

## Choosing the weight

Manufacturers generally suggest a blanket around 10% of your body weight. It is worth being clear that this is an industry convention, not a clinical guideline, and there is no strong research showing that 10% is better than 8% or 12%. Think of it as a starting point.

Some practical notes. If you are between sizes, go lighter; a blanket that is slightly too light still gives the sensation, while one that is too heavy is hard to move, hot and unpleasant to wake under. A blanket for a shared bed is a different calculation, because the weight is spread across two people and neither gets the full effect; most manufacturers advise a single-person blanket each, or a smaller blanket laid over one side. You should always be able to push the blanket off easily, lying down, without effort. If you cannot, it is too heavy for you.

## Fabric, fill and warmth

Fill is usually fine glass beads, which are dense, quiet and lie flat, or plastic pellets, which are bulkier and can rustle. Glass beads are generally the better choice for sleep. Look for small, closely stitched pockets so the beads cannot slide to one end and the weight stays even.

The cover decides how warm the blanket runs. Weighted blankets add insulation as well as weight, and a heavy quilted layer over a duvet can be too much for many UK bedrooms outside winter. Cotton and bamboo-blend covers breathe better than fleece or minky, which are cosy but hot. Some designs are sold as a weighted inner with a separate washable cover, which is far more practical than a single sealed blanket.

## Size: blanket versus bed

A weighted blanket should be sized to the person, not the bed. A single-size blanket that covers you from shoulders to feet gives the full pressure to one body. A double- or king-size weighted blanket spread across two people halves the effect for each and, because much of the weight hangs over the sides of the bed, tends to slide onto the floor in the night. Most manufacturers therefore suggest one blanket per person, or a single blanket laid over one side. Check the dimensions, not just the weight, before you buy.

## Washing and care

A blanket weighing 7 or 8 kg dry is heavier still wet, and many domestic washing machines are not rated for it. A removable cover you can wash normally is the answer; the weighted inner is then spot-cleaned or washed occasionally in a larger machine. Check the care label before buying, not after. Hang or lay it flat to dry; a tumble dryer may not cope.

## Who should avoid a weighted blanket

This is the section that matters most. A weighted blanket is not suitable for:

- **Babies, toddlers and young children.** Weighted products must never be used with a baby or in a cot, and for older children only with advice from a health professional. A child must always be able to remove it unaided.
- **Anyone with a breathing condition** such as asthma, COPD or sleep apnoea, or with any condition where extra pressure on the chest could be a problem, unless a doctor has said otherwise.
- **People with limited mobility or strength,** including some older adults and people with certain neurological or muscular conditions, who might not be able to move the blanket off if they became too hot or uncomfortable.
- **Anyone with poor circulation, diabetes-related nerve damage, or fragile skin,** where prolonged pressure is a concern.
- **People who overheat at night,** for whom any extra layer will make things worse; see [best temperature for sleep](@sleep-environment/best-temperature-for-sleep) before adding weight.
- **Anyone who feels claustrophobic or panicky under pressure.** The point is comfort; if it does not feel comforting, stop.

If you have any long-term condition and are unsure, ask a GP, pharmacist or occupational therapist before buying. And if sleep problems are persistent, [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep) sets out what is worth raising.

## Getting used to one

Give it a couple of weeks, and start with the blanket over your legs and torso for the first twenty minutes rather than the whole night. Many people find the first night oddly intense and the third night ordinary. Use it instead of a duvet in a warm room, or under a light duvet in a cold one, rather than on top of a winter tog. If you wake tangled, sweating or sore, that is information: try a lighter weight or a cooler cover before giving up on the idea, and stop altogether if it never feels like comfort.

## Who it does suit

Adults who find pressure calming, who already pile on blankets for comfort rather than warmth, who settle better when held, or who have used deep-pressure techniques before and liked them. People whose bedtime problem is a restless, unsettled feeling in the body rather than a stream of thoughts. Anyone with a cool bedroom and a partner who does not mind a heavy layer on one side of the bed.

## Free alternatives that give a similar feeling

You can test whether pressure helps you before buying anything. Tuck the duvet firmly under the mattress on both sides so it holds you snugly. Lay a folded heavy blanket or a second duvet across your chest and legs for the first twenty minutes, then push it off. Try [progressive muscle relaxation](@relaxation/progressive-muscle-relaxation-for-sleep), which uses tension and release to give the body the same heavy, settled feeling from the inside. A firm hug from a partner, or lying on your front for a few minutes, gives a version of the same sensation.

If the calm you are after is mental rather than physical, the [bedtime relaxation techniques](@relaxation/bedtime-relaxation-techniques) guide compares the approaches with the best evidence, none of which weighs anything.

## Where to go next

Try the tucked-duvet test for a week. If firm pressure helps you settle, and none of the cautions above applies, a glass-bead blanket around 10% of your body weight with a washable breathable cover is a sensible first purchase.
