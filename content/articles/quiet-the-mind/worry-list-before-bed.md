A worry list before bed is one of the few sleep techniques that is both simple and well grounded. The idea is that most bedtime worrying is unfinished thinking: things you have not decided, replied to or planned. Your mind raises them at night because it does not trust you to remember them. Writing them down, with a next step for each, gives it a reason to stand down.

The version with the best track record is called constructive worry. It is used in cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and it differs from a plain brain dump in two ways: every worry gets a concrete next action, and the writing happens in the early evening, not in bed.

This guide gives you the method, the timing, a template you can copy onto any piece of paper, and what to do when a worry turns up at 2am anyway.

## Why writing it down works

Worry has a job: to keep a problem in view until it is dealt with. When you are lying in the dark, it does that job by rehearsing. The problem is that rehearsal at night never reaches a conclusion, because there is nothing you can act on, so the loop repeats.

Putting the worry on paper makes it external, so your mind no longer has to hold it. Adding a next step turns an open loop into a plan, however small, and a worry with a plan attached is much easier to put down.

A small study by Colleen Carney and William Waters, published in *Behavioral Sleep Medicine* in 2006, found that a structured problem-solving exercise like this in the evening reduced pre-sleep mental arousal in people with insomnia. The evidence base is modest, but the technique is cheap and quick.

> **In short:** Write each worry down early in the evening with one realistic next step, then trust the list.

## The method, step by step

### 1. Pick a time that is not bedtime

Do this at least two hours before bed, at the kitchen table or on the sofa, never in bed; the bedroom needs to stay a place where worrying does not happen. After dinner is a natural slot.

### 2. Set a limit

Ten to fifteen minutes is enough; set a timer if you tend to spiral. The aim is a finished list, not a tour of every problem in your life.

### 3. Write the worry

Use two columns. On the left, write whatever is on your mind, one item per line. Keep each one specific and short: "the meeting with Priya on Thursday", "the boiler noise", "Mum's hospital letter". Include small things; they are often what surface at midnight.

### 4. Write the next step

On the right, write the single next action for each item. Not the whole solution, just the next move. "Read the agenda tomorrow morning." "Ring the plumber at lunch." "Ask Mum on Sunday what the letter said." If there is genuinely nothing to do, write that: "Nothing until they reply." That is still a decision.

### 5. Close it

Read the list once, fold it or close the notebook, and leave it in the room where you wrote it. Then move on with your evening; the [bedtime routine for adults](@falling-asleep/bedtime-routine-for-adults) guide has a full wind-down if you want one.

## A template you can copy

| What is on my mind | Next step, and when |
|---|---|
| Thursday meeting | Read the agenda at 8.30 tomorrow |
| Boiler noise | Ring the plumber in my lunch break |
| Mum's letter | Ask her about it on Sunday |
| Overdraft | Check the balance on Friday when I'm paid |
| Nothing I can name, just unease | Nothing to do tonight; notice it and let it sit |

The last row is deliberate. Sometimes the feeling has no object, and writing that down is more honest, and more settling, than hunting for one.

## What to do when a worry surfaces in bed anyway

It will happen, especially at first. Three responses, in order:

**Check whether it is already on the list.** Usually it is. Tell yourself, plainly, "That's written down. It has a step. It's for tomorrow." Then return to your breathing or to a neutral mental task. Our guide to [cognitive shuffling](@quiet-the-mind/cognitive-shuffling) is a good one for this moment, because it fills the space a worry would otherwise take.

**If it is new, park it in one line.** Keep a scrap of paper and a pen by the bed. Write three or four words, without turning on a bright light, and lie back down. Do not write the next step; that is for tomorrow's session.

**If it keeps coming back, get up.** After twenty minutes of lying awake and tense, go to another room, keep the lights low, and do something dull until sleepy. Lying in bed worrying, night after night, teaches your brain that this is what bed is for.

## Mistakes that make it less effective

- **Doing it in bed.** This is the most common one, and it undoes the point.
- **Writing feelings instead of items.** "I feel overwhelmed" is true but has no next step. Break it into the things that are overwhelming you.
- **Writing the whole plan.** One next step per item. More than that becomes a work session.
- **Re-reading it at midnight.** The list is finished when you close it. Checking it again is worrying in disguise; see our guide on [how to stop overthinking at night](@quiet-the-mind/how-to-stop-overthinking-at-night) for more on that.
- **Judging it on night one.** Give it a week.

## When a list is not enough

If your worries are constant, hard to control, frightening rather than practical, or follow you into the day, a worry list is a helpful habit but not a treatment. Talk to a GP, or in England refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies. If the thing you mostly worry about is sleep itself, our guide to [sleep anxiety](@quiet-the-mind/sleep-anxiety) addresses that particular loop.

## Tonight's version

After dinner, ten minutes, two columns, one next step each. Close the notebook, leave it in the kitchen. In bed, if something surfaces, remind yourself it is written down and give your mind something dull to do instead.
