If you can't sleep on Sunday night but drop off fine most other nights, you are not imagining a pattern. Sunday is the night when two separate problems land at once. Your body clock has drifted later over the weekend, so you are not actually sleepy at your weekday bedtime. And your mind has started the working week early, running through Monday's inbox before your head reaches the pillow.

Both problems have plain causes and plain fixes. You do not need to abandon lie-ins entirely. You need to keep the weekend drift within limits and move the Monday thinking to a time when it can do some good. This guide explains both and sets out a weekend plan most people can keep.

## Cause one: your weekend moved your body clock

Most of us sleep on a different schedule at the weekend: later nights on Friday and Saturday, then lie-ins, often two hours later than usual. This is sometimes called social jet lag, because it has the same effect on the body as flying a couple of time zones west and back again on Monday morning.

The body clock takes its main cue from when you wake and see light. Wake at 10am on Sunday instead of 7am and your clock shifts later, so by Sunday night you are not sleepy at 11pm; your body thinks it is nearer 9pm. You go to bed anyway, because it is Sunday, and lie there. You are not necessarily anxious; you are simply not ready.

The same drift also means that on Sunday you have been awake for fewer hours by bedtime, so less sleep pressure has built up. A late Sunday afternoon nap, common after a big lunch, takes off more.

## Cause two: Monday arrives on Sunday evening

The second mechanism is anticipatory anxiety: worrying in advance. Sunday evening is the first quiet moment since Friday, and for many people it is when the week ahead comes into view. The meeting, the commute, the unanswered messages, the sense of freedom ending.

It is what a mind does with unstructured time and an approaching deadline, and it affects sleep because worry raises arousal, the thing that has to fall for sleep to arrive. Add a body clock that is already running late, and Sunday night becomes reliably bad. Bad Sunday nights then teach you to expect them; the guide to [anxiety at night](@quiet-the-mind/anxiety-at-night) explains how that loop works.

> **In short:** Sunday night is a later body clock meeting an early start to Monday's worry. Fix the clock with your wake time and the worry with a Sunday afternoon plan.

## A weekend plan that doesn't ruin the weekend

### Keep your wake time within an hour

This is the single change with the most effect. If you get up at 7am on weekdays, aim for no later than 8am at the weekend. You can still go to bed later on Friday and Saturday; the wake time is what anchors the clock. Our guide to [why a consistent sleep schedule matters](@sleep-habits/consistent-sleep-schedule) goes into the reasoning.

If you cannot give up the lie-in entirely, make Sunday's wake time the earlier of the two. Sunday morning decides Sunday night.

### Get outside in the morning

Light after waking is what actually sets the clock. A walk, or twenty minutes by a bright window, on both weekend mornings helps hold your clock at its weekday setting.

### Watch the Sunday afternoon nap and the Sunday roast

A nap after 3pm, or longer than about twenty minutes, takes sleep pressure off the evening. Keep it short and early; the guide to [napping and night sleep](@sleep-habits/napping-and-night-sleep) explains the timing. Similarly, a very large, late lunch, or a couple of drinks with it, can leave you sleepy at 5pm and wide awake at midnight. Alcohol in particular fragments the second half of the night, as our guide to [alcohol and sleep](@sleep-habits/alcohol-and-sleep) sets out.

### Do the Monday thinking on Sunday afternoon

Rather than letting the week ambush you at 10pm, give it a slot at 4pm or 5pm. Fifteen minutes with a notebook: what is happening this week, what needs doing first, what you are dreading and what the first small step is. This is the [worry list method](@quiet-the-mind/worry-list-before-bed), just moved to a Sunday afternoon. Check your work calendar once if you must, then close it. Do not open email.

The point is that when the Monday thoughts arrive in bed, and they will for a while, you can honestly answer that they are already dealt with.

### Give Sunday evening something of its own

Giving Sunday evening a fixed, pleasant shape helps: a particular meal, a walk, a film you have seen before, a bath. Keep the last hour dim, and keep the news and the work phone out of it.

### Go to bed when sleepy, not because it is Sunday

If you have had a lie-in and are not sleepy at your usual time, going to bed anyway and lying there is the worst option. Stay up in low light doing something quiet until your eyes are heavy, even if that is later than usual. Then get up on Monday at your normal time, however the night went. One slightly short Monday is what resets the clock.

## If Sunday nights are part of something bigger

For most people this is a scheduling problem with some dread mixed in, and the plan above sorts it within a few weeks. Look further if:

- the dread on Sunday is intense, or you feel low or anxious about work most of the week
- you sleep badly most nights, not just Sundays
- the pattern has persisted for months despite a steady wake time

The first may be about the job rather than the sleep, and is worth talking through with someone you trust or a GP. The others are reasons to see a GP or, in England, to refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies. If the difficulty is specifically the night before an important Monday, our guide to [sleeping the night before a big day](@falling-asleep/cant-sleep-before-a-big-day) is about that.

## This weekend's version

Alarm no more than an hour later than weekdays, both days. Outside in the morning light. Short nap or none. Fifteen minutes with a notebook on Sunday afternoon. Dim, calm evening, bed when sleepy, and up at the usual time on Monday whatever happens.
