You are tired. You have been tired since about three o'clock. Yet now the light is off, your body is heavy and your brain is somewhere else entirely, running through tomorrow, replaying today, half-composing an email. You can't switch off at night, and the more tired you get, the more awake you feel.

The short explanation is that tiredness and alertness are run by different systems, and they can disagree. The need for sleep has built up all day, as it should. But the system that keeps you alert has not stood down, because nothing in your evening told it to. Most of us go from a screen, a work thread or a gripping episode straight into the dark, and expect the brain to change gear in the thirty seconds it takes to plug the phone in.

It won't. But it will change gear if you give it a transition. This guide explains the "tired but wired" state and describes a wind-down that acts as a proper off-switch.

## Why your brain won't shut down

Think of two dials. One is sleep pressure: it rises steadily from the moment you wake and is what makes you yawn on the sofa at nine. The other is arousal: how activated your nervous system is. Sleep happens when the first dial is high and the second is low.

When you can't switch off, the second dial is stuck up. Common reasons:

- **Work that ends at bedtime.** Finishing a report, checking messages, or even just thinking about tomorrow's meeting keeps the problem-solving parts of the brain busy, and they do not stop on request.
- **Stimulating screens.** It is less about the blue light than the content. News, social feeds, group chats and anything with a plot all keep attention hooked and arousal up. Our guide to [screens before bed](@sleep-habits/screens-before-bed) looks at what the evidence actually shows.
- **Late caffeine, late exercise, late sugar.** Each is a mild stimulant that lingers longer than people expect.
- **Bright light in the evening.** Overhead lighting and a bright bathroom keep the body clock in daytime mode and delay the release of melatonin, the hormone that signals night.
- **No gap.** Even a calm evening ends badly if the last thing you do is intense. The brain needs a buffer to move from doing to resting.

There is a second, sneakier reason. If you have had several nights of lying awake, bed itself starts to trigger alertness. You walk in, lie down and your brain thinks, "Right, this is where we do the thinking." That learned link is the reason the wind-down below happens partly out of the bedroom.

> **In short:** You're tired but wired because sleep pressure is high while arousal is still up. The answer is a transition, not more effort.

## Is this the same as insomnia?

Not necessarily. Having trouble switching off for a few nights during a busy or stressful period is normal, and it usually settles when the period ends. If it has been most nights for more than a few weeks, and you are struggling during the day, that pattern is closer to insomnia, and it is worth reading [why you might be tired but unable to sleep](@sleep-questions/why-am-i-tired-but-cant-sleep) and considering a chat with a GP. The wind-down still helps in both cases; persistent problems just usually need it alongside something more structured.

## A wind-down that sends the signal

The aim of the next hour is to move both dials in the right direction: let sleep pressure keep rising while bringing arousal down. Nothing here requires equipment or discipline beyond deciding when the day ends.

### 1. Decide the day is over, and mean it

Pick a time, roughly an hour before you want to be asleep, and treat it as the end of the working day even if the working day happens at home. Close the laptop. Put the phone on charge in another room, or at least out of arm's reach. If there is a task you genuinely cannot leave, write it on tomorrow's list and let the list hold it.

If your mind is full, this is the moment to empty it on paper. Ten minutes of [writing a worry list](@quiet-the-mind/worry-list-before-bed), each item with a next step, gives the brain permission to stop rehearsing. Do it at the kitchen table, not in bed.

### 2. Dim everything

Turn off the big light. Use lamps, ideally warm and low. Keep the bathroom light off if there is a window or a nightlight. This sounds cosmetic; it is not. Light is the strongest signal the body clock has, and an evening in dim light tells it that night has arrived.

### 3. Do something mildly boring with your hands

The buffer works best when it is low-key and slightly tedious. Wash up. Sort tomorrow's clothes. Water plants. Fold things. Have a shower or bath, which also helps by warming and then cooling the body, a change that is associated with falling asleep more easily. Stretch gently. Read a paper book that you have read before.

What you are avoiding is anything that demands a decision or produces a reaction: emails, news, arguments, a new box set, online shopping.

### 4. Choose one calm practice

The last ten to fifteen minutes before bed is a good slot for something that actively lowers arousal. It does not matter much which; it matters that you do the same one most nights so it becomes a cue. Slow breathing with a long out-breath. A body scan. Progressive muscle relaxation. A short guided relaxation with the screen face down. The [Relaxation guides](@relaxation) walk through each with a script.

### 5. Into bed only when sleepy

Sleepy is different from tired. Tired is heavy and fed up; sleepy is eyelids drooping and losing the thread of the page. Go to bed for the second one. If you get in and your brain starts up anyway, give it twenty minutes, then get up, keep the lights low, and come back when sleepy. This protects the link between bed and sleep.

## Quick fixes for the moment you are lying there

If you have skipped the wind-down tonight and are already in bed with a running mind:

- Slow your breathing, out-breath longer than in-breath, for a couple of minutes.
- Pick a neutral word and picture a random object for each letter, one after another, until you lose track.
- Turn the clock away and do not calculate how much sleep you will get.
- Try steady sound, which gives attention somewhere to rest.

## If silence is part of the problem

For some people the tipping point is not thoughts at all but the sudden quiet. A silent bedroom leaves every small noise, from the heating clicking on to a neighbour's door, standing out, and each one snaps attention back. A low, even background sound can smooth this over. [White noise](@sleep-sounds/white-noise-for-sleep) is the best known; brown noise, rain and fan sounds do the same job with a deeper tone that many people find easier to sleep to. The evidence that sound improves sleep overall is modest, but as a way of stopping small noises from jolting a mind that has just settled, it is a reasonable thing to try.

## When to get more help

Speak to a GP if you cannot switch off most nights for more than a few weeks, if you feel anxious or low during the day, or if the not-switching-off comes with unusually high energy and needing far less sleep than normal. Our guide to [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep) covers what to expect and what to mention.

## Tonight's version

Pick a time when the day ends. Phone in another room, big lights off, ten minutes on paper if your head is full, then something dull with your hands. Ten minutes of slow breathing, and bed when sleepy rather than when tired. It will feel strange for a few nights and then it will start to feel like a switch.
