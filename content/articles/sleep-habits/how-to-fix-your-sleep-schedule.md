To fix your sleep schedule, do one thing first: choose a wake time and keep it every day, including weekends. Then move it earlier (or later, if that is what you need) by no more than 15 to 30 minutes every few days, get bright light as soon as you wake, and keep the evening dim. Bedtime sorts itself out once sleepiness arrives at the new time.

That is the whole method. It is slower than the all-nighter people try in desperation, but it works and it lasts, because it moves the thing that actually controls your timing: your body clock.

This guide walks through it week by week, then covers the situations where the standard plan needs adapting, such as shift work, exam season, and a clock that seems set to 3am no matter what you do.

## Why bedtime is the wrong place to start

Most people who want to reset their sleep pattern try to go to bed earlier. It rarely works, because you cannot make yourself sleepy by lying down. Sleepiness is decided by two things: how long you have been awake, and what time your body clock thinks it is. If you have been getting up at 11am, your clock is not going to produce sleepiness at 10pm, however tired you feel of feeling tired.

Wake time is different. You can control it directly with an alarm, and it drives both of the things that produce sleepiness. Getting up earlier lengthens the day, so sleep pressure is higher by evening. And seeing light soon after waking pulls the body clock earlier. Do that for a week and the bedtime that seemed impossible starts to happen on its own.

> **In short:** You cannot force bedtime, but you can set wake time. Fix the morning and the evening follows.

## The week-by-week reset

This plan assumes you want to move earlier, which is by far the more common request. If you need to move later, for example after a period of very early starts, simply reverse the light advice: dim mornings, bright evenings.

### Before you start: pick the target

Decide the wake time you actually need on a working day and the amount of sleep you usually feel best on (for most adults it is somewhere between seven and nine hours). Count back from the wake time to get a rough target bedtime. Do not aim for more sleep than you need; extra time in bed is time spent awake.

Then note where you are now. If you have been waking at 10.30am and want 7am, that is a three-and-a-half-hour shift, which will take two to three weeks. Knowing that in advance stops you giving up on day four.

### Week one: anchor and shift

- Set your alarm for 30 minutes earlier than your current natural wake time. Get up when it goes off, every day, weekends included.
- Within an hour of waking, get outside for ten to twenty minutes, or sit by the brightest window you have. Cloud does not matter; daylight outdoors is still far brighter than indoor lighting.
- Have breakfast and, if you drink it, your coffee, at the same time each morning. Regular meals are a secondary time cue for the clock.
- Every three days or so, if you are managing the current wake time, move it another 15 to 30 minutes earlier.
- In the evening, do not try to go to bed at the target time yet. Go when you feel sleepy. Dim the lights from about two hours before your intended bedtime and keep screens dim or away.
- No naps after 2pm, and no naps longer than 20 minutes.

By the end of the first week you will typically have moved 60 to 90 minutes, and the evening sleepiness will have started arriving earlier.

### Week two: hold the gains and keep moving

Continue the same pattern. This is the week the plan usually gets abandoned, because the novelty has gone. Two things help. First, a bright, pleasant morning routine you actually look forward to, which our guide to [how your morning routine shapes tonight's sleep](@sleep-habits/morning-routine-for-better-sleep) covers. Second, treating the weekend as two more days of the plan rather than a reward. A Sunday lie-in of two hours will undo most of the week's progress.

If you hit a wall at a particular time (many people find 7am fine but 6.30am impossible), stop shifting and hold the time you can manage for a week before trying again.

### Week three and beyond: settle in

Once you are waking at the target time and falling asleep within about twenty minutes of the target bedtime, stop moving. Now the job is to keep it, which is easier than getting there. Your wake time can flex by about half an hour without harm; more than an hour, regularly, and the drift starts again. There is more on holding a routine in our guide to [why a consistent sleep schedule matters](@sleep-habits/consistent-sleep-schedule).

## The two tools that do most of the work

Everything in the plan comes down to two levers.

### Light

Light is the strongest signal your body clock receives. Bright light in the morning pulls your clock earlier; bright light in the evening pushes it later. That is the entire basis of the plan, and it is why the outdoor morning light is not optional. If you cannot get outside, a bright window is the next best thing.

In the evening the aim is the reverse. Keep overhead lights off and use lamps, and in the last hour keep screens dim or put them down. If your bedroom is not dark, because of a streetlamp, a summer dawn or a partner who reads late, deal with that too: [making your bedroom darker](@sleep-environment/how-to-make-bedroom-darker) runs through the options from cheap to permanent.

A sleep mask is the quickest fix while you are resetting. Early summer light at 4.30am is one of the most common reasons a new schedule slips, because it wakes you before the alarm and then you are tired all day. A mask holds the darkness until the alarm. Our [guide to sleep masks](@sleep-products/best-sleep-masks-uk) explains the types.

### Sleep pressure

The second tool is simply being awake long enough. If you nap in the afternoon or doze on the sofa at 8pm, you release the pressure you have spent all day building, and the target bedtime is lost. Stay upright and lightly occupied in the evening. If you go to bed at the new time and are not sleepy, do not lie there for an hour; our guide to [how to sleep when you're not tired](@falling-asleep/how-to-sleep-when-not-tired) covers what to do instead.

## The all-nighter myth

Staying up for a whole night to "reset" is popular because it sounds decisive. It does not work. An all-nighter builds enormous sleep pressure, so you will certainly crash the next evening, but it does nothing to your body clock, which is still set to the old time. Most people sleep heavily for one night and then drift straight back. If you have already tried it, no harm done; just start the gradual plan from wherever you are.

The same goes for trying to "catch up" with a marathon lie-in at the weekend. It relieves the debt a little but pushes the clock later, which is the opposite of what you want. Our guide to [whether you can catch up on sleep](@sleep-questions/can-you-catch-up-on-sleep) explains what does and does not carry over.

## Special cases

The standard plan assumes you can choose your wake time. Not everyone can.

### Shift workers

If your hours rotate, a fixed wake time is not available to you, and the honest answer is that your clock will never fully adjust to nights. What helps: keep the same sleep timing across a run of the same shifts, use a mask and earplugs to sleep in daylight, get bright light at the start of a night shift and wear sunglasses on the way home, and avoid caffeine in the second half of the shift. On days off, decide whether to stay on night timing or switch back, rather than doing half of each. If shift work is leaving you unable to function, that is worth a conversation with your GP or occupational health.

### Students and long holidays

A term of 3am bedtimes is a big shift to undo, and the mistake is to leave it until the night before term starts. Begin the reset ten days to two weeks before you need the new timing. Morning light matters even more for younger adults, whose clocks naturally run later.

### A clock that is set late

Some people have a body clock that is genuinely and persistently set late, so that they cannot fall asleep before 2 or 3am and cannot wake before late morning, however hard they try. This is sometimes called delayed sleep phase and it is not laziness. The gradual method still helps, but if you have followed it carefully for a month and are still stuck, it is worth talking to a GP, who may refer you for specialist advice on light timing. Our guide to [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep) covers what to say.

## When a "schedule problem" is really something else

If your timing is fine but you lie awake anyway, or you fall asleep easily and wake at 3am, the plan above is not the answer. That pattern is more likely to be insomnia, for which the recommended first-line treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). The rest of [the Sleep Habits guides](@sleep-habits) and the Waking at Night collection will get you closer to what is going on.

## The plan on one page

- **Choose** a target wake time and bedtime; work out how far you have to move.
- **Set** the alarm 30 minutes earlier than now, seven days a week.
- **Light** within an hour of waking, outdoors if possible; dim from two hours before bed.
- **Shift** another 15 to 30 minutes every three days, or hold when it gets hard.
- **Protect** sleep pressure: no naps after 2pm, none over 20 minutes, no dozing on the sofa.
- **Bed** when sleepy, not by the clock, and get up if you are wide awake.
- **Hold** the wake time within half an hour once you arrive, weekends included.
