If you want to sleep better tonight, the most useful hour of the day is the first one after you wake. What you do then, and especially how much light you see, tells your body clock what time it is. Roughly fifteen to sixteen hours later, that same clock decides when you feel sleepy. A morning routine for better sleep is not about cold showers or journalling; it is about giving the clock a clear, consistent signal.

The morning is also the easiest lever to pull. Evening habits ask you to resist things: the phone, the extra episode, the glass of wine. Morning habits mostly ask you to do one or two small things at a time of day when your willpower is fresh. Here is what matters and why.

## Why the morning matters so much

Your body clock runs slightly long, a little over 24 hours for most people, so it needs resetting every day. Light is what does it. Bright light in the morning nudges the clock earlier; light in the evening pushes it later. Without a strong morning signal the clock drifts, sleepiness arrives later each night, and mornings get harder in a slow spiral.

The morning light signal also starts a timer. Melatonin, the hormone that signals night, begins to rise roughly fourteen to sixteen hours after your first exposure to bright light. See daylight at 7am and the sleepy feeling is due around 10 or 11pm. See it for the first time at midday, through a window, and it is due much later. Our guide to [why a consistent sleep schedule matters](@sleep-habits/consistent-sleep-schedule) covers the clock in more detail; this one is about what to do with the first hour.

> **In short:** Morning light starts the countdown to tonight's sleepiness. Get it early and consistently, and the evening looks after itself.

## The four things that matter

### 1. Get up at the same time

A fixed wake time is the foundation of everything else here. It decides when the light lands and how long you will have been awake by bedtime. Keep it within about half an hour every day, including weekends. If mornings are a struggle, put the alarm across the room, and resist the second alarm: dozing between snoozes is light, fragmented sleep that leaves you groggier, not more rested.

If you wake well before the alarm and cannot get back to sleep, that is a different problem, and our guide to [waking up too early](@waking-at-night/waking-up-too-early) is the one to read.

### 2. See daylight within the first hour

This is the single most effective thing in this article. Ten to twenty minutes outdoors soon after waking, or thirty on a dull day, gives the clock the signal it needs. It does not have to be sunny: even an overcast British morning is many times brighter than a well-lit room, and light through a window is much weaker than light outside.

Ways to fit it in: walk part of the way to work, have your tea in the garden or on the step, walk the dog before breakfast, or open the curtains and stand by the window while the kettle boils if going out is not possible. In winter, when it is dark until eight, take the light when it comes and consider a brighter bulb in the kitchen for the meantime. Our guide to [bedroom lighting for sleep](@sleep-environment/bedroom-lighting-for-sleep) covers why the reverse applies in the evening.

### 3. Move a little

Exercise is a secondary time cue, and combining it with daylight is ideal. This does not need to be a workout. A brisk ten-minute walk, some stretching, or cycling to work all count. Morning movement also raises body temperature and alertness in a way that reinforces the "day has started" signal, and regular activity improves sleep quality over time. There is more on what the research says in [exercise and sleep](@sleep-habits/exercise-and-sleep).

### 4. Put caffeine where it does no harm

Morning is the right time for coffee or tea. The half-life of caffeine averages about five hours, so a cup at 8am is largely gone by bedtime, while the same cup at 4pm is not. There is a popular idea that you should delay the first coffee by an hour or two to avoid interfering with the natural morning rise in alertness; the evidence for that is thin, and if the first cup is part of a routine you enjoy, keep it. What matters much more is when the last one is, which we cover in [caffeine and sleep: how late is too late](@sleep-habits/caffeine-and-sleep).

## Things that help, but less

**Breakfast at a regular time.** Meal timing is a mild cue for the clock. Eating within an hour or two of waking, at a similar time each day, adds to the signal. Skipping breakfast is not a sleep problem in itself.

**A shower.** A warm or cool shower makes many people feel more awake, but it does little for the body clock. Fine if you like it; not a sleep tool.

**Not reaching for the phone first.** This matters less for the clock than for your state of mind. Ten minutes of news and messages in bed starts the day in a hurry. If the phone charges outside the bedroom, this takes care of itself.

## What a realistic morning looks like

Alarm at the same time as yesterday. Up, curtains open, kettle on. Tea by the back door or a short walk round the block, ten minutes, whatever the weather. Breakfast at roughly the usual time. That is the whole routine, and it takes no longer than the morning you already have.

If you are trying to move your whole schedule earlier, the same routine is the engine of it, applied to a wake time you shift gradually; our [week-by-week guide to fixing your sleep schedule](@sleep-habits/how-to-fix-your-sleep-schedule) shows how.

## The short version

Tomorrow: get up at the same time as today, and get outside for ten minutes within the first hour. Do that every day for two weeks and notice when sleepiness arrives in the evening. It is the smallest change in [the Sleep Habits guides](@sleep-habits) with the largest effect.
