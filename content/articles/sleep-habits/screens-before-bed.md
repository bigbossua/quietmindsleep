Screens before bed do affect sleep, but the honest answer is more useful than the headline. Light from a phone or laptop does send a wake-up signal to your body clock, and that can delay sleepiness. But in most studies the effect of the light on its own is modest. What tends to do more damage is the content, which keeps your mind engaged and alert, and the drift, where "five more minutes" becomes an hour and bedtime lands much later than planned.

That matters because it changes the fix. Turning on night mode addresses the smallest of the three problems. Deciding what you do on the phone in the last hour, and when you put it down, addresses the other two.

This guide goes through each of the three effects, what the evidence actually supports, and a few rules that people manage to keep, which is the only kind worth having.

## Three ways a screen gets in the way of sleep

### 1. The light

Your body clock is set by light reaching special cells in the eye, and those cells are most sensitive to blue-ish light, which screens produce plenty of. In the evening, light tells your brain it is still daytime, delays the rise of melatonin (the hormone that signals night), and can push sleepiness later.

The effect is real, and it has been shown in laboratory studies. But those studies often used bright screens held close to the face for hours, or compared a backlit e-reader at full brightness with a paper book. In ordinary use, a phone at a sensible brightness in a dim room produces a much smaller signal than, say, sitting under bright kitchen lights, which most people do not worry about. Light matters, and it is worth reducing, but it is rarely the main reason a phone habit wrecks a night.

### 2. The content

This is the part the "blue light" framing misses. Scrolling news, replying to a work message, watching one more episode, or losing an argument on a group chat all keep your mind in daytime mode. They raise alertness, and alertness is the opposite of what you need to fall asleep. A calm article or a familiar sitcom does far less harm than a feed designed to keep you refreshing it. The screen is the same; the effect on your brain is not.

Content also follows you into bed. If the last thing you read was upsetting or engaging, that is what your mind will chew on when the light goes off. If that is your pattern, our guide to [why you can't switch off at night](@quiet-the-mind/cant-switch-off-at-night) picks up where this one leaves off.

### 3. The delay

The simplest effect is the biggest. A phone in bed makes it very easy to go to sleep an hour later than you intended, night after night. That hour is lost sleep, plainly, and it also nudges your body clock later, so that the next night you are not sleepy at the old time either. Over a few weeks a screen habit can quietly move your whole schedule, which is why phones feature in most guides to [fixing a drifted sleep schedule](@sleep-habits/how-to-fix-your-sleep-schedule).

> **In short:** The light is the least of it. A screen mostly harms sleep by keeping your mind busy and by stealing the hour you meant to be asleep.

## What about night mode and blue light glasses?

Warmer screen settings (Night Shift, night light, reading mode) reduce the amount of blue-ish light and are worth switching on in the evening. Dropping the brightness does more than changing the colour. Together they take the light problem down to something minor.

The claim that a filter alone will noticeably improve sleep is not well supported. Trials of night-mode settings and of blue-light-blocking glasses have produced mixed results, with several finding no meaningful difference. That is consistent with the light being the smaller part of the problem: you can make a phone amber and dim and still stay up until one in the morning arguing with a stranger.

## Rules people actually keep

Blanket bans ("no screens after 8pm") tend to last about four days. These are more realistic.

**Change what, not just whether.** In the last hour, move from feeds, news and work to things that are calm and finite: a downloaded episode of something you have seen before, a podcast, an audiobook, a book. Finite matters. A chapter ends; a feed does not.

**Put the phone somewhere that is not the bed.** Charging it across the room, or in another room, is the single most effective change most people make, because it removes the 11.40pm scroll and the 3am check in one go. A cheap alarm clock does the alarm's job.

**Dim everything, not just the screen.** Turn the overhead lights off from about an hour before bed and use lamps. The whole room is a light source, not only the phone. Our guide to [bedroom lighting for sleep](@sleep-environment/bedroom-lighting-for-sleep) covers bulbs and lamps in more detail.

**Set a stopping point that is not "when I'm tired".** Tiredness is a poor signal when you are looking at something engaging. Pick a time, or the end of an episode, and decide beforehand that this is the point you put it down.

**Keep the bed for sleep.** If you want to read, watch or scroll, do it on the sofa or in a chair, then move to bed when you feel sleepy. It keeps the bed associated with sleep, which pays off on the nights you are struggling.

**Use the phone for something that helps.** A phone can play a guided relaxation, a sleep story or steady background sound, all of which help some people. The rule is that it goes face down and you do not touch it again. If that sounds like a bedtime routine, it is; our guide to [a bedtime routine for adults](@falling-asleep/bedtime-routine-for-adults) shows how to put it together.

## Reading before bed

Reading is the most common replacement for the phone, and it is a good one. A paper book is ideal. A dim e-reader is nearly as good. Reading on a phone, at low brightness with night mode on, is acceptable if what you are reading is calm. The point is the same as above: the content and the stopping point matter more than the device. We look at this in more detail in [does reading before bed help you sleep](@sleep-questions/does-reading-before-bed-help-you-sleep).

## Teenagers and screens

Adolescent body clocks naturally run later, and teenagers are more sensitive to evening light, so the same habits cost them more. If you are a parent trying to negotiate this, the most useful battle is the phone leaving the bedroom overnight, not a total evening ban. NHS advice for young people's sleep makes the same point.

## The short version

Do two things tonight: turn night mode and low brightness on from early evening, and charge the phone outside the bedroom or across the room. Then, for the last half hour before bed, swap the feed for something calm with an end. That covers all three problems, and it is a habit you can keep.
