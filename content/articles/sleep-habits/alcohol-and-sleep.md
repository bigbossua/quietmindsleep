Alcohol does help you fall asleep. That is not a myth: it is a sedative, and after a couple of drinks most people drop off faster than usual. The trouble comes later. As the alcohol wears off during the night, sleep becomes lighter and more broken, dreaming (REM) sleep is suppressed, and you are more likely to wake hot, thirsty, needing the toilet, or simply wide awake at 3am. The night is longer at the start and worse in the middle.

This is not about whether you should drink. It is about understanding what a drink does to the night so you can decide when, and how much, is worth it to you. If you regularly have a glass of wine to help you sleep, the honest news is that it is probably costing you more sleep than it gives.

## Sedation is not sleep

The reason a nightcap feels like it works is that alcohol acts on the same brain systems that sedative medicines use. It slows things down, quietens a busy mind and shortens the time it takes to fall asleep. For the first couple of hours you may even get slightly more deep sleep than usual.

But sedation and sleep are not the same thing. Sleep is an active process with a structure: cycles of light sleep, deep sleep and REM, repeated four or five times through the night in a particular order. Alcohol disrupts that structure. It gets you to the front door of sleep quickly and then rearranges the furniture inside.

The other problem is tolerance. If you use alcohol as a sleep aid regularly, the sedative effect fades within a few nights, so you need more to get the same drop-off, while the disruption to the rest of the night does not fade at all.

> **In short:** A drink makes falling asleep easier and staying asleep harder. The first half of the night is bought at the expense of the second.

## What happens in the second half of the night

Your body processes alcohol at roughly one unit an hour, so a few drinks at 10pm are mostly cleared by 2 or 3am. As the level drops, several things happen more or less together.

**Rebound wakefulness.** As the sedation lifts, the brain swings the other way: lighter sleep, more brief awakenings and a general sense of being on alert. This is the mechanism behind the classic 3am wake after drinking, and it is why people so often say they "slept badly" after a night out even though they were unconscious in minutes. Our guide to [waking up at 3am](@waking-at-night/waking-up-at-3am) covers the other reasons the small hours are so common for waking.

**Suppressed REM.** Alcohol cuts REM sleep, the stage in which most dreaming happens and which matters for memory and mood, particularly in the first half of the night. Later, as the alcohol clears, REM can rebound with vivid, sometimes unpleasant dreams. Waking from those is part of why a hungover morning feels so unrested.

**Bathroom trips.** Alcohol suppresses the hormone that normally reduces urine production overnight, and you have probably drunk a fair amount of liquid. Two trips to the loo at 2am and 4am each cost you a stretch of sleep and give your mind a chance to start up.

**Heat and sweating.** Alcohol widens blood vessels near the skin, which makes you feel warm and can leave you sweating in the night. A warm body is a poor sleeper. If this is a familiar pattern, [waking up hot at night](@waking-at-night/waking-up-hot-at-night) has practical ways to run cooler.

**Snoring and breathing.** Alcohol relaxes the muscles in the throat, narrowing the airway. Snoring gets louder, and for people with sleep apnoea the pauses in breathing become more frequent. A partner will often notice this before you do; if you are on the receiving end, our guide to [sleeping with a snoring partner](@sleep-environment/how-to-sleep-with-a-snoring-partner) may help.

**The morning after.** Poorer sleep plus the direct effects of alcohol leave you tired, and for some people anxious. The low mood and jitteriness the day after drinking ("hangxiety") is partly a sleep problem and partly the brain's rebound from the sedation.

## How much and how late matter

Two things decide how much of the night is affected: how much you drink and how close to bed you drink it.

**Timing.** Because the body clears about a unit an hour, a drink with dinner at 7pm is largely gone by the time you sleep at 11. The same drink at 10.30pm is still being processed at 2am. Moving your drinking earlier in the evening is the single most useful change if you do not want to drink less.

**Amount.** The disruption scales with dose. One glass of wine has a small effect on most people's sleep; three or four produce the full pattern described above. Sleep studies find measurable REM suppression and fragmentation at moderate doses, and it becomes obvious at higher ones.

**Regularity.** A nightly drink is more of a problem for sleep than the same total spread over a weekend, because the sleep disruption becomes the norm and tolerance builds to the sedative part. It can also quietly become the thing you rely on to switch off, which is worth noticing.

## Practical ways to limit the damage

- **Finish earlier.** Aim for your last drink at least three hours before bed. With dinner is ideal.
- **Alternate with water.** It slows the pace, reduces the dehydration and takes the edge off the morning. It does mean more bathroom trips, so front-load it earlier in the evening.
- **Keep the room cool** and use lighter bedding on a night you have been drinking, since you will run warmer.
- **Do not add a sleeping tablet or antihistamine on top.** Combining sedatives is unsafe, and neither will fix the second-half disruption.
- **Expect the 3am wake and plan for it.** If you do surface, do not lie there frustrated. Our guide to [getting back to sleep when you wake in the night](@waking-at-night/cant-get-back-to-sleep) has a calm sequence to follow.
- **Try a week off and compare.** The clearest demonstration is your own: a week of alcohol-free nights, judged by how you feel at 7am rather than by how quickly you dropped off.

## If alcohol has become the way you sleep

Plenty of people slip into a routine where a drink is the only reliable way to wind down, and that is understandable, especially during a stressful patch. The difficulty is that it tends not to work for long and it keeps the underlying problem unaddressed. If the real issue is a mind that will not settle, there are better tools: our guide to [calming your mind before bed](@quiet-the-mind/how-to-calm-your-mind-before-bed) is a good place to start, and for persistent insomnia the recommended treatment is CBT-I rather than anything in a bottle.

The NHS suggests keeping to no more than 14 units a week, spread over three or more days, and Drinkaware has straightforward, non-judgemental tools for keeping track. If you are finding it hard to cut down, or you are drinking to get through the night, that is a good reason to talk to your GP; they hear it often and will not be surprised.

## Tonight's version

If you are drinking this evening, have your last one with dinner rather than at bedtime, keep a glass of water by the bed, and open a window. If you usually have a nightcap to help you sleep, try skipping it for a week and see how 7am feels. That single experiment tells you more than any article, including this one, and it fits neatly alongside the other changes in [the Sleep Habits guides](@sleep-habits).
