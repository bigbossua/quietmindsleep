You cannot make yourself sleepy by lying down, and trying to sleep when you are not tired is one of the most reliable ways to lie awake. The useful question is why you are not tired at bedtime, because there are really only two answers: your body clock thinks it is earlier than the clock on the wall, or you have not built up enough pressure to sleep. Each has a different fix.

The short answer for tonight: if you are genuinely alert, stay up in low light doing something quiet until you feel heavy-eyed, then go to bed. Get up at your usual time tomorrow regardless. Over the following week, fix the clock with morning light and dim evenings, and protect the sleep pressure by not dozing in the day.

This guide explains both mechanisms and what to do about each. It is one of our [Falling Asleep guides](@falling-asleep).

## "Not tired" and "not sleepy" are different things

Tired is a whole-body feeling: drained, heavy, worn. Sleepy is specific: eyes heavy, attention slipping, yawning, the pull towards sleep itself. You can be exhausted and not sleepy, which is the frustrating state of lying in bed with a body that aches for rest and a brain that is fully lit. And you can be sleepy without feeling especially tired, which is what happens on a good evening.

Only sleepiness gets you to sleep. So when you say you are "not tired" at bedtime, what usually matters is that you are not sleepy, and sleepiness comes from two systems working together.

## The two systems that make you sleepy

**Sleep pressure** builds through every hour you are awake. A chemical called adenosine accumulates in the brain and is cleared by sleep. The longer you have been up, the stronger the pull, which is why a long day ends in heavy eyes and a lazy day often does not.

**Your body clock** (the circadian rhythm) sets the time of day at which that pressure is allowed to turn into sleepiness. In the evening it produces a signal that rises through the night. It is set mainly by light, especially morning light, and by when you get up.

You feel sleepy when both line up: high pressure and a clock that says it is night. If you are not tired at bedtime, one of them is out of step.

## Reason 1: your clock is running late

If you feel fine at 11pm, come alive at midnight and would happily sleep until 10, your clock is probably set later than your schedule. This is common in teenagers and young adults, in people who spend evenings in bright light, in anyone who has had a run of late nights or a lie-in weekend, and in some people as a lifelong tendency. The evening "second wind" many people describe is often this: the clock's alerting signal peaking a couple of hours before its own bedtime, which happens to be later than yours. Our guide to [why you get a second wind at night](@sleep-questions/why-do-i-get-a-second-wind-at-night) explains that pattern.

**Signs:** you sleep well at weekends when you go to bed later; you feel wide awake rather than tense; mornings are the hard part.

**What helps:** morning light, within an hour of getting up, ideally outside. A fixed wake time seven days a week. Dim evenings: lamps not overheads, warm bulbs, and screens turned down. Bedtime moved earlier in small steps of 15 minutes every few nights rather than in one jump. Our guide to [bedroom lighting for sleep](@sleep-environment/bedroom-lighting-for-sleep) covers the evening side. If the whole schedule has drifted, the [week-by-week schedule reset](@sleep-habits/how-to-fix-your-sleep-schedule) is the structured version.

## Reason 2: you have not built enough sleep pressure

If you are going to bed at a normal time and simply not sleepy, look at where the pressure went.

**Naps.** A doze on the sofa after dinner, even 20 minutes, clears enough adenosine to leave you alert at 11. So does a long afternoon nap. Our guide to [napping and night sleep](@sleep-habits/napping-and-night-sleep) explains how to nap without paying for it.

**Too much time in bed.** If you spend nine hours in bed to get six hours of sleep, the pressure is spread thin. Sleep clinics often shorten the time in bed for a while so that the pressure concentrates. It sounds backwards and it works.

**Too little activity.** A day spent mostly sitting builds less pressure than a day with a walk in it. This is not about exhausting yourself; a brisk 30 minutes, ideally outside, is enough to make a difference.

**Going to bed too early.** Sometimes the answer is simply that your body is not ready yet. If you went to bed early to catch up on a bad night, you have almost certainly arrived before the sleepiness has.

> **In short:** Not tired at bedtime means either the clock is late or the pressure is low. Morning light and a fixed wake time fix the first; no dozing and a full day fix the second.

## Should you go to bed anyway?

Generally, no. Going to bed alert and lying there teaches your body that bed is a place for being awake, and after a few weeks of that the bed itself starts to wake you up.

The better option is to wait for sleepiness in low light. Stay up, but stay quiet: a book, a familiar audiobook, a slow task. Avoid anything bright or engaging. Go to bed when your eyes are heavy, even if that is later than planned. Then, and this is the part that makes it work, get up at your normal time. You will be short of sleep for a day, which builds more pressure for the next night, and the pattern corrects itself within a few days.

The exception is if you are calm and comfortable and simply not asleep yet. Resting quietly in the dark has some value, and if you are not frustrated there is no harm in staying. The cue to get up is tension, not the clock.

## What not to do

Do not use alcohol to bring sleepiness on. It works in the short term and fragments the night. Do not lie in tomorrow, because that moves the clock later and repeats the problem. Do not try relaxation techniques as a way to force sleep; they help a tense body settle, but they cannot create sleepiness that the clock and the pressure have not supplied.

And do not decide, after one alert night, that something is wrong with you. Most people have evenings like this. If it is most evenings, and it takes you well over half an hour to fall asleep for weeks on end, the guide to [how long it should take to fall asleep](@falling-asleep/how-long-should-it-take-to-fall-asleep) explains where the line is and when it is worth talking to a GP.

## Where to go next

Tonight: stay up in dim light until you are heavy-eyed, then go to bed. Tomorrow: up at the usual time and outside within the hour. This week: no dozing after mid-afternoon, and lamps rather than the big light after 9pm. If the problem is the clock, expect a week or two before it shifts.
