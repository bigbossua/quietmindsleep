Earplugs are better for steady, loud noise you want gone: traffic, a motorway, a fan next door, a partner's snoring. White noise is better for irregular noise you cannot predict: doors, footsteps upstairs, a barking dog, the bins at 6am. Earplugs are cheaper and work anywhere; white noise is easier to live with and helps everyone in the room, not just you.

That is the short answer to earplugs vs white noise. The reason both exist is that they do different jobs. Earplugs *reduce* the sound that reaches your ear. White noise *hides* it, by raising the background so that sudden sounds stand out less. Understanding that difference tells you which one you need, and whether you need both.

## What each one actually does

**Earplugs** physically reduce sound. A well-inserted foam plug takes something like 25–30 decibels off, which turns a busy road into a distant hum. They work on everything, but they work best on higher-pitched, steady sounds. Low rumble, such as bass through a wall or heavy lorries, comes through the bones of the skull and the pillow as much as through the ear canal, so plugs reduce it less.

**White noise** does not reduce anything. A steady sound at a moderate volume makes your brain less sensitive to changes in sound, and it is the change, not the volume, that wakes you. A door slamming in a silent room is a jolt; the same door with a fan running is barely noticeable. Masking is most effective against sounds at a similar or lower volume than the noise itself, which is why it handles footsteps and voices well and a car alarm under the window less well. There is more on the different types of steady sound in the [white noise for sleep guide](@sleep-sounds/white-noise-for-sleep).

> **In short:** Earplugs turn the volume down. White noise turns the contrast down. Steady noise wants earplugs; sudden noise wants white noise.

## Earplugs vs white noise at a glance

| | Earplugs | White noise |
|---|---|---|
| **Best for** | Steady, loud noise: traffic, snoring, hum | Sudden, irregular noise: doors, neighbours, dogs |
| **How much noise removed** | Substantial reduction with a good fit | None removed; sounds are made less noticeable |
| **Low rumble (bass, lorries)** | Partly; travels through bone and pillow | Poor; masking struggles with low frequencies |
| **Comfort** | Varies a lot; foam can ache, silicone or wax suits some better | No physical contact; some people dislike the sound |
| **Side sleepers** | In-ear plugs can press against the pillow; wax or short plugs help | No issue |
| **Partner** | Only helps the wearer; partner unaffected | Helps or annoys both; volume is a shared decision |
| **Hearing an alarm or a child** | Reduced; most alarms still audible, vibrating alarms remove doubt | Unaffected; you hear alarms and a crying child normally |
| **Cost** | Pence per pair for foam; £10–£30 for reusable | Free with a fan or app; £20–£60 for a machine |
| **Travel** | Ideal | Portable machines or an app work |
| **Long-term use** | Fine with clean hands and regular replacement; earwax can build up | Fine at moderate volume; some people come to rely on it |
| **Habituation** | A few nights to get used to the feel | A few nights to get used to the sound |

## When to choose earplugs

- The noise is loud and continuous, and you want as much of it gone as possible.
- You sleep alone, or your partner does not want a sound running all night.
- You travel, work shifts, or sleep in different places.
- You are on a tight budget: foam plugs cost pence.
- The noise is a snoring partner, where masking alone often is not enough.

The main failure is fit. Foam plugs need to be rolled tight, inserted while pulling the top of the ear up and back, and held for a few seconds while they expand. Poorly inserted plugs do half the job and fall out. If in-ear plugs ache, mouldable wax or silicone putty sits over the canal instead of inside it. ## When to choose white noise

- The noise is irregular: neighbours, footsteps, a lift, a dog, doors.
- You cannot stand anything in your ears, or you are a side sleeper who finds plugs press on the pillow.
- You need to hear a baby, a child, or an alarm without any doubt.
- You share the room and want a solution that helps both of you.
- You find total silence unsettling, which is common; silence makes every small sound larger.

The main failure is volume. People turn white noise up to fight a loud sound and end up with a bedroom that is louder than the problem. Keep it around the level of a quiet conversation, place the source a couple of metres from the bed rather than beside your head, and leave it on all night so that it does not stop and wake you. A fan-based machine gives a natural sound without a loop; digital machines offer softer, deeper options that some people find easier to sleep to. The [white noise machine buying guide](@sleep-products/best-white-noise-machines-uk) explains what to look for.

## Using both

Very noisy bedrooms, such as a flat above a bar or a bedroom on a main road, often need both. The plugs bring everything down; the white noise fills in the low background that the plugs let through and covers the sudden sounds that would otherwise stand out in the new quiet. Keep the machine at a normal volume; the point of the plugs is that you need less noise, not more.

Sleep headphones are a middle path: small in-ear sleep earbuds give a little blocking and some masking, and a soft headband keeps the sound at the ear without plugs.
