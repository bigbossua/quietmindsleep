Generally, yes. Sleeping with earplugs every night is not known to damage hearing and is common practice among people who live near roads, share a bed with someone who snores, or simply sleep lightly. The problems that do come up are practical rather than serious: earwax build-up, sore or irritated ear canals, and the question of whether you would hear a smoke alarm, a child or the front door. All three can be managed with a few habits.

Here is what can go wrong, how to avoid it, and the signs that mean a trip to the pharmacist or GP.

## The things that can go wrong

**Earwax.** Ears clean themselves by moving wax slowly outwards. An earplug pushed into the canal every night can push wax back in and compact it, and some people find that nightly plugs lead to a blocked feeling, muffled hearing or occasional discomfort. The NHS has clear guidance on earwax build-up; the short version is that it is common, usually harmless and often clears on its own or with a few days of olive oil drops from a pharmacy. Do not use cotton buds to clear it, with or without earplugs.

**Irritation and infection.** A plug that is dirty, damp or too large can rub the skin of the canal, and a warm, slightly moist ear canal is a good place for bacteria and fungi to settle. This is more likely with foam plugs that are reused for too long, or silicone plugs that are never washed. Pain, itching, discharge or a feeling of fullness are the signs to watch for.

**Not hearing what you need to hear.** A well-fitted foam plug reduces sound by somewhere between twenty and thirty decibels. That takes traffic and snoring down to a murmur, but it also takes the edge off an alarm clock, a smoke alarm on the landing, or a child calling from another room. Most people still hear a loud alarm through plugs, particularly one in the same room, but "most" is not "all".

**Dependence.** Not physical, but habitual. Some people find that after months of plugs, ordinary night-time sounds feel intrusive when they sleep without them, for instance in a hotel. This is a preference rather than a harm, and it passes within a few nights.

> **In short:** Nightly earplugs are fine for most people. Keep them clean, replace foam ones often, do not force them in, and check that you can still hear your alarms.

## How to use them safely

**Insert gently.** For foam plugs: roll the plug into a thin cylinder, reach over your head with the opposite hand and pull the top of the ear up and back to straighten the canal, slide the plug in and hold it for twenty or thirty seconds while it expands. It should sit in the canal, not be crammed deep into it. If it hurts, it is in too far or the plug is too big.

**Take them out slowly.** Twist gently to break the seal before pulling. Yanking a plug out can be uncomfortable and, in a canal full of wax, unhelpful.

**Keep them clean.** Foam plugs are cheap because they are meant to be replaced; a pair a week, or sooner if they look grubby or stop springing back, is a reasonable rule. Silicone and other reusable plugs should be washed in warm soapy water and dried fully before going back in. Never share plugs.

**Consider a lower level of blocking.** If you are a side sleeper who finds in-canal plugs uncomfortable, or you worry about missing sounds, mouldable wax or silicone putty plugs sit over the entrance to the canal rather than inside it. They block a little less, which is sometimes exactly what you want. Our guide to [foam, silicone or wax earplugs](@sleep-products/types-of-earplugs-for-sleep) compares the trade-offs.

**Test your alarms.** With the plugs in, have someone set off the smoke alarm briefly, or at least sound your alarm clock from across the room. If you cannot hear it, use a lower-attenuation plug, a vibrating alarm or a bed-shaker.

**Give your ears a night off now and then.** Not essential, but it lets you notice any build-up or soreness before it becomes a problem.

## Who should be more careful

Parents of young children, carers, anyone responsible for hearing a night-time call, and people who live alone and rely on a smoke alarm to wake them should think carefully about how much sound they block. A lighter plug, one ear only, or [steady background sound instead of plugs](@sleep-environment/earplugs-vs-white-noise) may be a safer balance.

People who already get frequent ear infections, have had ear surgery, wear hearing aids, or have a perforated eardrum should ask a GP or pharmacist before starting nightly earplugs.

## When to see a pharmacist or GP

Ear pain, itching that does not settle, discharge, a persistent blocked feeling, ringing in the ears or noticeably reduced hearing all warrant a conversation. A pharmacist is the right first stop for a suspected wax build-up or mild irritation; a GP is the right stop for pain, discharge or anything that has lasted more than a week or two. Do not put drops in an ear that is painful or discharging without advice.

## Choosing plugs for nightly use

If you are going to wear them every night, comfort and hygiene matter more than raw noise blocking. Softer, slower-expanding foam or washable silicone suits most side sleepers; wax putty suits people who cannot tolerate anything in the canal.
