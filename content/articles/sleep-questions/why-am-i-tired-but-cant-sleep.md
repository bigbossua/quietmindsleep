The short answer is that "tired" and "sleepy" are two different things, and only one of them opens the door to sleep. Tired is what your body and mind feel after a long day: depleted, heavy, fed up. Sleepy is a specific signal from the brain that it is ready to switch off. You can have a great deal of the first with none of the second, and that is the state people describe as tired but wired.

Four things usually explain the gap. Your alert system is still running, because of stress, worry or a mind that has not been told the day is over. Your body clock is not ready yet, so you are trying to sleep at the wrong time for you. You have pushed past the point of sleepiness and got a second wind. Or something you have taken in, usually caffeine, is blocking the sleepy signal from getting through.

This page explains each in plain language, then gives you something to do tonight and something to change this week.

## Tired versus sleepy

Two systems decide when you sleep. The first is sleep pressure: a chemical called adenosine builds in the brain across the waking day and is cleared during sleep. The longer you are awake, the stronger the pressure. The second is your body clock, which sends an alerting signal that rises through the day and drops away in the evening.

You fall asleep easily when sleep pressure is high and the alerting signal has faded. Feeling tired tells you the first condition is probably met. It says nothing about the second. If the alerting signal is still high, whether because of stress, timing or stimulants, you get exhaustion without sleepiness.

Being tired is also not purely a sleep matter. Mental effort, emotional strain, physical illness and low mood all produce tiredness, and none of them necessarily produces sleepiness. A day of difficult meetings can leave you drained and, at the same time, too keyed up to sleep.

> **In short:** Tiredness is the need for rest. Sleepiness is the readiness for sleep. You can lie awake feeling wrecked because your brain has one without the other.

## Reason 1: your alert system is still on

Sleep specialists use the word hyperarousal for a nervous system that stays in daytime mode at night. Heart rate a little higher, muscles a little tighter, thoughts quicker. It is the body's response to anything it reads as unfinished business: an argument, a work problem, a worry about money, or the worry about not sleeping itself.

Tiredness makes this worse, not better. A tired mind is less able to let go of a thought, so the same three worries go round on a loop with nothing to interrupt them. If this sounds familiar, our guide to why you [can't switch off at night](@quiet-the-mind/cant-switch-off-at-night) goes into the mechanism and the practical fixes properly.

## Reason 2: it is the wrong time for your body clock

If you are naturally a late type, or a run of late nights has pushed your clock later, then going to bed at 10:30pm because you feel shattered may be asking yourself to sleep two hours before your brain is ready. The tiredness is real. The sleepiness has not arrived.

Your clock also sends a burst of alertness in the last couple of hours before your natural sleep time. Many people who feel exhausted at 8pm and strangely awake at 10:30pm are simply feeling that burst. We cover this in more detail in [why you get a second wind at night](@sleep-questions/why-do-i-get-a-second-wind-at-night).

## Reason 3: you are overtired

Anyone who has looked after a small child knows that a toddler kept up too long does not fall asleep faster. Adults are the same, only quieter about it. When you push through strong sleepiness, say to finish a piece of work, the body responds with a small release of stress hormones to keep you going. By the time you get to bed, you are running on that instead.

The sign is a wave of sleepiness earlier in the evening that you ignored, followed by feeling oddly alert an hour later. The fix is boring but effective: notice the first wave tomorrow, and go with it.

## Reason 4: something is blocking the signal

Caffeine works by sitting in the receptors that adenosine, the sleep-pressure chemical, would normally use. The pressure is still building; you just cannot feel it. Half of a cup of coffee is still in your body five or six hours later, so a 4pm coffee is a measurable presence at 10pm. Tea, energy drinks, cola and some painkillers all count. Our guide to [caffeine and sleep](@sleep-habits/caffeine-and-sleep) covers cut-off times.

Screens matter less for the light they give off than for what they do to your attention. A phone in bed keeps the mind engaged and delays the moment you actually try to sleep, and scrolling through news or work messages hands your alert system fresh reasons to stay up. Our honest look at [screens before bed](@sleep-habits/screens-before-bed) separates the evidence from the alarm.

Alcohol is a special case: it makes you feel sleepy and helps you drop off, then breaks up sleep later. Nicotine is a stimulant. Some prescribed medicines can also affect sleep, and if you suspect yours does, ask a pharmacist rather than changing anything yourself.

## What to do tonight

**Do not go to bed until you are actually sleepy.** Not tired: sleepy. Heavy eyes, yawning, losing the thread of what you are reading. If that means bed is an hour later than usual, so be it, provided you get up at your normal time.

**Spend the wait in dim light doing something dull.** A paper book, a podcast you have heard before, a slow tidy-up of one drawer. Nothing with a screen, nothing with a deadline.

**Give your body a task instead of a goal.** Trying to sleep keeps the alert system on. Slow breathing gives it something to do while sleep arrives on its own. A simple pattern from our [breathing exercises for sleep](@relaxation/breathing-exercises-for-sleep) is enough: breathe out for longer than you breathe in, and keep going for a few minutes.

**If you are lying awake and getting annoyed, get up.** Go to another room, keep the lights low, and come back when sleepy. Bed should be associated with sleep, not with staring at the ceiling.

If tonight is a genuine "not tired at all" night rather than a "tired but wired" one, our guide to [how to sleep when you're not tired](@falling-asleep/how-to-sleep-when-not-tired) takes a slightly different approach.

## What to change this week

1. **Hold one wake time**, every day, with daylight soon after. This resets the clock and moves the evening alertness burst earlier over a week or two.
2. **Move caffeine to before lunch.** For a week. Then decide.
3. **Put a boundary on the day.** Half an hour before bed, write down what is unfinished and what you will do about it tomorrow. Then close the notebook.
4. **Catch the first wave.** When sleepiness arrives in the evening, go to bed then, not after one more episode.
5. **Wind down in dim light.** Lamps rather than the big light, screens down or at least away from the bed.

## When tired but wired is something else

Feeling exhausted but unable to sleep for a few nights is common and usually passes when whatever caused it passes. If it goes on for more than a few weeks, if you are exhausted all day despite what looks like enough sleep, or if low mood, panic or a racing heart come with it, it is worth a conversation with your GP. Persistent tiredness has causes that have nothing to do with sleep habits, from anaemia to thyroid problems, and they are easy to check. Our guide on [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep) explains what to expect.

## The short version

You are tired but not sleepy, and only sleepy gets you to sleep. Tonight, wait for sleepiness in dim light and give your body a breathing pattern to do instead of a goal. This week, fix the wake time and move the caffeine.
