If you are reading this at 3am, here is the short answer. Put the phone down after this paragraph, turn any clock away, and lie still with your eyes closed for a while, breathing slowly and letting yourself rest without trying to sleep. If you are still wide awake and frustrated after what feels like 20 minutes, get up, sit somewhere dim and do something dull until you feel sleepy, then go back to bed.

That sequence is the whole method. The rest of this guide explains each step, why it works, and what to do if the usual reason you can't get back to sleep is your own mind.

## Why getting back to sleep is harder than falling asleep

At bedtime you have a full day's worth of sleep pressure behind you. By the middle of the night most of it has been spent, so there is less pulling you under. Your body has also begun its slow preparation for morning, with core temperature and alertness hormones starting to rise. Waking at 3am is easy; falling back is harder. That is not a fault in you, and it is worth knowing before you start blaming yourself.

The things that turn a brief waking into an hour awake are almost always the same: checking the time, doing arithmetic about how much sleep is left, lying there trying hard, and thinking. So the method is built around not doing those.

## Step 1: don't check the time

The clock is the single most reliable way to lose the next hour. Knowing it is 3.14am tells you nothing useful and gives your mind two things to do: calculate how little sleep you will get, and note that it is the same time as last night. Both wake you further.

Turn the clock to the wall, or move it. Put your phone face down and out of reach. If you do not know the time, you cannot worry about it.

## Step 2: stay dim

Light in the early hours tells your body clock that morning has come. Keep the room dark, and if you must get up, use the dimmest light you can. Do not turn on the big light, and do not open your phone: the screen is bright, and whatever is on it is more interesting than sleep.

## Step 3: rest without trying to sleep

Lie in a comfortable position, close your eyes and stop trying. Trying to sleep is a form of effort, and effort is the opposite of what sleep needs. Give yourself permission to simply rest. Resting in the dark with your eyes closed is genuinely restorative, and it is the state from which sleep most often returns.

If it helps to have something to do, choose from these:

**Slow your breathing.** Breathe in gently, then let the out-breath be a little longer than the in-breath. You do not need to count precisely. A longer out-breath signals to the body that it is safe to settle. The [breathing exercises for sleep](@relaxation/breathing-exercises-for-sleep) guide has several patterns, but any slow, easy rhythm will do at 3am.

**Do a body scan.** Start at your feet and move your attention slowly upwards, noticing each part and letting it soften. It occupies the mind with something neutral and it relaxes the body at the same time. There is a full script in [body scan for sleep](@relaxation/body-scan-for-sleep).

**Give your mind something boring.** Count down from 300 in threes. Walk through a familiar route in detail. List towns beginning with each letter of the alphabet. The point is to occupy the thinking part of your brain with something that has no emotional pull.

**Listen to something quiet.** A calm audiobook, a low-key podcast you have heard before, or steady sound such as rain or brown noise, played through headphones that are comfortable to lie on, can give a busy mind somewhere to rest. Keep the volume low and choose something you will not mind missing the end of.

> **In short:** Dark, still, eyes closed, breathing slow, mind gently occupied. Rest is the goal; sleep is a side effect.

## Step 4: if it isn't working, get up

If you have been lying there long enough to feel frustrated, wide awake or tense, staying in bed is now working against you. Every minute spent lying awake and annoyed strengthens the link between your bed and being awake and annoyed. Sleep specialists call breaking that link "stimulus control", and it is one of the most effective parts of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia.

The rule of thumb is about 20 minutes, judged by feel rather than by clock. Get up, put on something warm, and go to another room, or at least to a chair. Keep the light low. Then do something genuinely dull: a paperback you have read before, a crossword, folding laundry, sitting with a warm drink (not tea or coffee). No screens, no work, no cleaning the kitchen. The aim is low stimulation, not productivity.

Go back to bed only when you feel sleepy: heavy eyelids, yawning, the sense that you could nod off. Not merely bored. If you get back into bed and are wide awake again after a while, get up again. It can take a few nights for this to bite, but it works because it retrains the association. The full reasoning, and the exceptions, are in [should you get up if you can't sleep](@waking-at-night/should-i-get-up-if-i-cant-sleep).

## When the problem is thinking

For many people, the reason they can't get back to sleep is not the room or the body but a mind that treats 3am as a planning meeting. Worries feel larger in the dark, solutions feel further away, and every train of thought seems urgent.

A few things help. Remind yourself, plainly, that nothing gets solved at 3am and that this is a known effect of the hour, not a true reading of the situation. If a specific worry will not leave, write it down on a pad by the bed, in the dark if necessary, with a note to deal with it tomorrow. And if you are lying there thinking about sleep itself, how little you are getting and how tomorrow will go, that is its own trap, covered in [sleep anxiety](@quiet-the-mind/sleep-anxiety).

If waking with a pounding heart or a sense of dread is your pattern, rather than ordinary worry, see the guide to [waking up with anxiety in the night](@waking-at-night/waking-up-with-anxiety), which handles that carefully.

## Things that don't help

- **Trying harder.** Sleep is not something you can do; it is something you allow.
- **Staying in bed for hours "at least resting".** Calm rest is fine. Tense wakefulness is not rest, and it teaches the wrong lesson.
- **Checking your phone "just for a second".**
- **Getting up and starting the day at 4am.** Unless it is close to your usual wake time, this leaves you short and makes tomorrow night's waking more likely.
- **A drink to get back off.** Alcohol fragments sleep further and you will pay for it in an hour.

## Making it easier next time

The in-the-moment method works better if the night itself is set up well. Keep the room cool and dark. If sound is the trigger, deal with it: earplugs, or a steady sound that smooths over noise. If you lean on audio to settle a busy mind, headphones designed to be slept in are more comfortable than ordinary earbuds, and the [best sleep headphones](@sleep-products/best-sleep-headphones-uk) guide explains the options for side sleepers in particular.

Above all, get up at the same time every morning, whatever the night was like. It is the most reliable way to make wakings shorter and rarer, because it steadies the clock that decides when light sleep arrives.

## If this is happening most nights

An occasional broken night is normal life. If you are waking and struggling to get back to sleep most nights for more than a few weeks, and it is affecting your days, it is worth talking to a GP. Persistent insomnia is treatable, and CBT-I is the recommended first step. Early waking that comes with low mood, or wakings with loud snoring or gasping, are also worth raising. There is a plain guide to [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep), and the rest of the [Waking at Night guides](@waking-at-night) cover the specific patterns.

## Tonight's version

Clock away, phone away, lights off. Lie still, breathe slowly, let yourself rest. If it turns into frustration, get up, sit in the dim, do something dull, and come back when you are sleepy. Tomorrow, get up at the usual time regardless. That is enough for tonight.
