Waking up with anxiety in the night is a particular kind of awful. One moment you are asleep; the next your heart is pounding, your chest feels tight, your mouth is dry, and your mind has already found a reason: a work problem, a health worry, a vague conviction that something is wrong. Everything feels more serious than it would at midday.

Here is the first thing to hold on to. What you are feeling is adrenaline, your body's alarm system firing during a light stage of sleep. It is very unpleasant, it is very common, and it is rarely a sign of anything dangerous. It will pass in minutes if you let it. This guide explains why it happens, how to settle in the moment, and what to change so that it happens less. It also says plainly when it is worth seeing a GP.

If your heart is racing right now and you also have chest pain, pain spreading to your arm or jaw, breathlessness, or you feel faint, stop reading and call 999. Those need checking, every time.

## What is actually happening

Your body has a threat response, sometimes called fight-or-flight, that releases adrenaline to prepare you to act. It is fast, it is physical, and it does not need a real threat to fire; it only needs the brain to judge that something might be wrong.

In sleep, especially the lighter sleep of the second half of the night, several things can trip it:

- **Stress that has not switched off.** If your days are tense, your alarm system idles higher at night too, and a small disturbance is enough to set it off.
- **A dream.** You may not remember it, but a stressful dream in REM sleep can leave you waking with a pounding heart and no idea why.
- **Alcohol wearing off.** A drink in the evening is sedating at first, then produces a rebound of restlessness and a faster heart rate three to four hours later, as it is cleared. The mechanism is explained in [alcohol and sleep](@sleep-habits/alcohol-and-sleep).
- **Caffeine.** Still active in your system long after the last cup.
- **Being too hot, needing the toilet, or a drop in blood sugar** after a very light dinner. Any bodily discomfort can be read by a sleeping brain as a threat.
- **Breathing disturbance.** Some people with sleep apnoea wake gasping with a racing heart, and mistake it for anxiety.

Once adrenaline is flowing, the mind does what minds do at 3am: it looks for a reason. In the dark, with no distractions and no one to talk to, that reason is easy to find and hard to argue with. So the physical surge and the frightening thought reinforce each other, and a 30-second alarm becomes 40 minutes of dread.

> **In short:** The pounding heart comes first, the frightening thought comes second, and each feeds the other. Settling means breaking that loop, gently, from the body end.

## How to settle in the moment

The aim is not to argue yourself out of the worry. At 3am you will lose that argument. The aim is to bring the body down, and let the mind follow.

### 1. Name it

Say to yourself, plainly: "This is adrenaline. It's the alarm going off at the wrong time. It will pass." This is not a trick; it is accurate, and accuracy takes some of the fear out.

### 2. Lengthen the out-breath

Adrenaline speeds up your breathing, and fast, shallow breathing keeps the alarm going. Breathe in gently through the nose, then breathe out slowly, for longer than you breathed in, as if you were fogging a mirror. Repeat for a minute or two. You do not need to count precisely, though some people like a structure such as [box breathing](@relaxation/box-breathing) to hold on to.

### 3. Ground yourself in the room

Feel the weight of your body on the mattress. Notice the temperature of the air, the texture of the duvet, the sounds outside. Press your feet against the bed. Name five things you can feel. This pulls your attention out of the imagined future and back into the ordinary present, where nothing is actually happening.

### 4. Unclench

Adrenaline tenses muscles. Work through your jaw, shoulders, hands, stomach and legs, letting each go soft. Do it slowly; this doubles as a distraction.

### 5. Park the thought

If a specific worry keeps returning, write it on a pad by the bed, in a few words, in the dark if necessary. Tell yourself you will deal with it tomorrow, when you can think straight. This sounds too simple to work, and it works.

### 6. Rest, and get up if you must

Once the surge has passed, stay dark and rest without trying to sleep. If you find yourself wound up again after what feels like 20 minutes, get up, sit somewhere dim and do something dull until you are sleepy. The full sequence is in [what to do when you can't get back to sleep](@waking-at-night/cant-get-back-to-sleep).

## Why everything feels worse at night

It is worth understanding this, because it helps you distrust the 3am verdict. In the small hours the rational, planning part of the brain is less active, emotional processing runs hotter, and there is nothing to compete with the worry for your attention. You are also tired, which shrinks perspective. The problem you are catastrophising about at 3am is the same problem it was at 3pm; you are just less equipped to see it clearly. There is more on this in [anxiety at night](@quiet-the-mind/anxiety-at-night).

## Making it happen less

**Deal with the worry in daylight.** A few minutes each evening, well before bed, writing down what is on your mind and what the next step is for each item gives your brain less unfinished business to raise at night. The [worry list](@quiet-the-mind/worry-list-before-bed) approach is simple and well supported.

**Move caffeine earlier and alcohol out.** Both prime the alarm. Two weeks without an evening drink is the quickest experiment most people can run.

**Keep the room cool and dark.** Overheating is a common trigger that gets blamed on the mind.

**Keep a steady wake time.** It stabilises the body clock and makes light sleep, where these wakings happen, less fragmented.

**Learn a settling routine while calm.** Practise the breathing and grounding steps in the day, so that at 3am they are familiar rather than one more thing to try to remember.

**Talk to someone.** Anxiety that is waking you at night rarely stays in the night. If your days are anxious too, that is the thing to address, and the [Quiet the Mind guides](@quiet-the-mind) are one starting point.

## When to see a GP

Please make an appointment if:

- you are waking with intense fear, a pounding heart and a sense of doom repeatedly, especially if the episodes peak within minutes and leave you shaken, since these may be nocturnal panic attacks
- the night-time anxiety has lasted more than a few weeks or is part of anxiety you feel during the day
- you snore loudly, wake gasping or choking, or a partner has noticed you stop breathing
- you have palpitations that come with chest pain, breathlessness or dizziness, or that happen during the day too
- you are waking early with low mood, or you have started dreading going to bed

Panic attacks and anxiety disorders are common and respond well to talking therapies such as CBT, which you can refer yourself to through NHS talking therapies in England. A GP can also rule out physical causes such as thyroid problems or a heart rhythm issue. There is a broader guide to [when to see a GP about sleep](@sleep-questions/when-to-see-a-gp-about-sleep), and the rest of the [Waking at Night guides](@waking-at-night) cover the other patterns.

If you ever feel you cannot keep yourself safe, call 999, go to A&E, or call the Samaritans free on 116 123.

## Tonight's version

If you wake with your heart going: name it, lengthen the out-breath, feel the bed under you, let your muscles go soft, write the worry down, rest. It will pass. Tomorrow, look at the worry in daylight, and if this keeps happening, tell your GP.
