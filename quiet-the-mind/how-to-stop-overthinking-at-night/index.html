How to Stop Overthinking at Night

You cannot simply decide to stop overthinking at night, but you can change what you do with the thoughts. These steps give your mind somewhere else to go.

Overthinking at night is not a willpower problem. Telling yourself to stop thinking is like telling yourself not to picture a red bus: the instruction itself keeps the subject alive. What works instead is a set of small, practical moves that give the thoughts somewhere to go before bed, and give your attention something else to hold once you are lying down.

This guide is a step-by-step version of that approach. The first few steps happen in the evening, well before bed. The rest are for the moment when you are lying there and the thinking has started anyway. At the end there is a short list of things that feel helpful but tend to make overthinking worse.

If you want the background on why minds get louder after dark, start with our guide to racing thoughts at night. This one is about what to do.

Step 1: Give worry an appointment (early evening)

Most overthinking before sleep is unfinished business: things you have not decided, replied to, or resolved. Your mind raises them at night because night is the first quiet moment it has had.

The counter-move is to give that business a proper slot earlier on. Choose a fixed 15 minutes, ideally at the same time each evening and at least two hours before bed. Sit somewhere that is not your bedroom. Let yourself think about whatever is bothering you, deliberately, for the whole slot. When the time is up, stop.

This sounds strange, and it takes a week or two to feel natural. What it does is teach your brain that there is a time for this, and it is not 1am. When a thought surfaces in bed, you have an honest answer: "That's for tomorrow's slot."

Step 2: Write a worry list, with next steps

Worry time works better with pen and paper. Write down each thing on your mind, then next to it write the single next action, however small ("email Sam to ask", "check the bank balance on Friday", "nothing I can do until they reply"). The technique is called constructive worry and comes from cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). Our guide to writing a worry list before bed has a template and the details.

Two points matter more than the format. Do it early, not in bed. And write the next step, not just the worry; a list of problems without actions can leave you feeling worse.

Step 3: Put a buffer between the day and bed

Overthinking loves momentum. If the last thing you did before lying down was reply to a work message, watch something tense or read the news, your brain arrives in bed still running at that speed.

Give it 30 to 60 minutes of low-key, low-light activity. A shower, a paper book, some tidying, stretching, a boring podcast, a conversation. Dim the lights. Leave your phone charging somewhere other than the bedside table; the research on screens before bed is more nuanced than the headlines, but a phone within reach is an invitation to check things, and checking things feeds thinking.

In short: Deal with the day's thoughts on paper early in the evening, then spend the last hour doing something too dull to think hard about.

Step 4: In bed, change the task rather than the thought

Now the part that most people search for: you are lying there and the thinking has started regardless. The mistake is to try to stop the thought. The better approach is to replace the task. Here are three ways, from most active to most passive.

Cognitive shuffling

Pick an emotionally neutral word with five or six letters, such as "garden". For each letter in turn, picture as many unrelated objects starting with that letter as you can: gate, giraffe, gravel, glove. Hold each image for a moment, then move on. When you run dry, go to the next letter.

Cognitive shuffling works because it occupies the part of your mind that would otherwise be problem-solving, using material that leads nowhere. The evidence is limited to small studies, but it is free and many people find it surprisingly effective. If you prefer, count backwards from 300 in threes, or mentally walk through a route you know well.

Slow breathing

Thoughts run faster when the body is tense, and slower when it is not. A simple pattern is enough: breathe in through the nose for a count of four, out through the mouth or nose for a count of six or more, and repeat for a few minutes. The longer out-breath is what matters; it nudges the nervous system towards its resting state. If you would like something more structured, 4-7-8 breathing is a popular option, though the specific ratio has less evidence behind it than slow breathing in general.

Breathing exercises can backfire for a minority of people who become very aware of their breath and start monitoring it. If that is you, try shuffling or muscle relaxation instead.

Steady sound or a familiar voice

Silence gives thoughts a clear run. Low, constant sound, or a voice you have heard before, gives attention a soft place to land. Rain, brown noise, a fan, a well-worn audiobook chapter, a calm podcast you already know the ending of. The guide to calming sounds for anxiety at night goes through the options.

Keep it quiet enough to ignore, choose content with no cliffhangers, and use a sleep timer. New or exciting material has the opposite effect.

If one thought keeps coming back

Sometimes it is not a stream of thinking but a single loop: the same conversation, the same worry, replayed. Two small moves help here. The first is to name it, silently and plainly: "I'm having the thought that the meeting will go badly." Putting that frame around it creates a little distance; you are the person noticing the thought, not the thought itself. The second is to give it a deliberate parking place: "That's on the list. I'll look at it at seven tomorrow." Then return to your breathing or your shuffling word.

The loop will come back, probably several times. That is not failure. Each time you notice and return, you are practising the skill, and the returns get shorter.

Step 5: If you are still wired after 20 minutes, get up

This feels counter-intuitive when you are tired, but it is one of the best-supported parts of insomnia treatment. Lying in bed thinking for an hour teaches your brain that bed is where thinking happens. Getting up breaks that link.

Go to another room, keep the lights low, and do something dull until you feel genuinely sleepy. Then go back. The guide to whether you should get up if you can't sleep explains the reasoning and the practical details, such as what to do in a cold flat in January.

What not to do

These are the habits that feel like coping and quietly make overthinking worse.

Checking the time. Every glance gives your mind a new problem to calculate ("only five hours if I fall asleep right now"). Turn the clock to the wall. If your phone is your clock, that is another reason to leave it in another room.

Reaching for your phone. Scrolling feels like a distraction, and for the first two minutes it is. Then you have read a message that needs an answer, or a headline, and you have more to think about than before.

Trying harder to sleep. Effort and sleep do not mix. If you notice yourself straining, switch to one of the tasks above; the aim of each is to be occupied and relaxed, with sleep as a side effect.

Going over the list again. If you have done a worry list, trust it. Re-checking it in your head at midnight is overthinking wearing a disguise.

A drink to switch off. Alcohol makes falling asleep faster and the rest of the night worse, with more waking in the small hours, which is prime overthinking time.

Where sound and headphones fit

You do not need to buy anything to stop overthinking at night. If you find that a voice or steady sound helps and you share a bed, or you are a side sleeper who finds ordinary earbuds uncomfortable lying down, headphones designed for sleep are one practical option. When overthinking is something more

Everyone overthinks on a bad week. If it is happening most nights for several weeks, if the thoughts are frightening rather than busy, or if you feel anxious or low during the day as well, it is worth talking to a GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, which offer CBT for anxiety and, in some areas, for insomnia. Neither means you have a disorder; it means you have a pattern that a structured approach can help with. If the worrying has become mostly about sleep itself, see our guide to sleep anxiety.

Tonight's version

Tonight, do three things. Spend 15 minutes early this evening writing down what is on your mind and the next step for each item. Keep the final hour dim and dull, phone in another room. In bed, if thinking starts, pick a word and shuffle through it, and get up if you are still wired after 20 minutes.
