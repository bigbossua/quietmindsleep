Earplugs vs White Noise: Which Is Better for Blocking Noise at Night?

Earplugs remove noise; white noise hides it. Earplugs win for steady, loud sound and for people who sleep alone. White noise wins for sudden sounds, shared rooms and anyone who hates things in their ears. Plenty of people use both.

Earplugs are better for steady, loud noise you want gone: traffic, a motorway, a fan next door, a partner's snoring. White noise is better for irregular noise you cannot predict: doors, footsteps upstairs, a barking dog, the bins at 6am. Earplugs are cheaper and work anywhere; white noise is easier to live with and helps everyone in the room, not just you.

That is the short answer to earplugs vs white noise. The reason both exist is that they do different jobs. Earplugs reduce the sound that reaches your ear. White noise hides it, by raising the background so that sudden sounds stand out less. Understanding that difference tells you which one you need, and whether you need both.

What each one actually does

Earplugs physically reduce sound. A well-inserted foam plug takes something like 25–30 decibels off, which turns a busy road into a distant hum. They work on everything, but they work best on higher-pitched, steady sounds. Low rumble, such as bass through a wall or heavy lorries, comes through the bones of the skull and the pillow as much as through the ear canal, so plugs reduce it less.

White noise does not reduce anything. A steady sound at a moderate volume makes your brain less sensitive to changes in sound, and it is the change, not the volume, that wakes you. A door slamming in a silent room is a jolt; the same door with a fan running is barely noticeable. Masking is most effective against sounds at a similar or lower volume than the noise itself, which is why it handles footsteps and voices well and a car alarm under the window less well. There is more on the different types of steady sound in the white noise for sleep guide.

In short: Earplugs turn the volume down. White noise turns the contrast down. Steady noise wants earplugs; sudden noise wants white noise.

When to choose earplugs

  • The noise is loud and continuous, and you want as much of it gone as possible.
  • You sleep alone, or your partner does not want a sound running all night.
  • You travel, work shifts, or sleep in different places.
  • You are on a tight budget: foam plugs cost pence.
  • The noise is a snoring partner, where masking alone often is not enough.

The main failure is fit. Foam plugs need to be rolled tight, inserted while pulling the top of the ear up and back, and held for a few seconds while they expand. Poorly inserted plugs do half the job and fall out. If in-ear plugs ache, mouldable wax or silicone putty sits over the canal instead of inside it. The earplugs buying guide compares foam, silicone and wax, and whether it is OK to wear earplugs every night is covered separately.

When to choose white noise

  • The noise is irregular: neighbours, footsteps, a lift, a dog, doors.
  • You cannot stand anything in your ears, or you are a side sleeper who finds plugs press on the pillow.
  • You need to hear a baby, a child, or an alarm without any doubt.
  • You share the room and want a solution that helps both of you.
  • You find total silence unsettling, which is common; silence makes every small sound larger.

The main failure is volume. People turn white noise up to fight a loud sound and end up with a bedroom that is louder than the problem. Keep it around the level of a quiet conversation, place the source a couple of metres from the bed rather than beside your head, and leave it on all night so that it does not stop and wake you. A fan-based machine gives a natural sound without a loop; digital machines offer softer, deeper options that some people find easier to sleep to. The white noise machine buying guide explains what to look for.

Using both

Very noisy bedrooms, such as a flat above a bar or a bedroom on a main road, often need both. The plugs bring everything down; the white noise fills in the low background that the plugs let through and covers the sudden sounds that would otherwise stand out in the new quiet. Keep the machine at a normal volume; the point of the plugs is that you need less noise, not more.

Sleep headphones are a middle path: small in-ear sleep earbuds give a little blocking and some masking, and a soft headband keeps the sound at the ear without plugs.

Where to go next

If you have not tried either, start with a £3 pack of foam plugs and a fan you already own. A week of each tells you which one you can live with. Frequently asked questions

Will I hear my alarm with earplugs in?

Most people do. Foam earplugs reduce sound rather than removing it, and an alarm a metre from your head is loud. If you are worried, use a vibrating wrist alarm or put your phone alarm under the pillow. Try it on a morning when it does not matter first.

Can I use earplugs and white noise together?

Yes, and it is a common combination for very noisy bedrooms. The white noise fills in the low background that earplugs let through, and the earplugs bring everything down to a level where the white noise can cover it. Keep the machine at a moderate volume; you do not need to turn it up because you are wearing plugs.

Is it bad to sleep with white noise every night?

At a moderate volume, placed away from the bed, there is no good evidence of harm for adults. Some people find they come to rely on it, which is only a problem if you cannot take it with you. Keep the volume around that of a quiet conversation and it is a reasonable long-term habit.
